A full-day hike · Yosemite National Park, California

Looking down on Half Dome:
Clouds Rest from Tenaya Lake

Walk out along a bare granite ridge until Half Dome is {{u:300 m|a thousand feet}} below you and all of Yosemite Valley lies beyond it, with no permit and no lottery. It takes a whole day at altitude, and one hill has to be climbed again on the way home.

{{PHOTO:hero}}

Why this hike

The famous summit in Yosemite is Half Dome. To stand on it you need a permit won in a lottery, a very long day up from the valley floor, and a place in the line on its steel cables. Clouds Rest is the higher mountain next door, and it asks for none of that.

  • The better view. From Half Dome you cannot see Half Dome. From here you look down on it, along the whole of Tenaya Canyon and into Yosemite Valley, with the high country behind you.
  • A high start. The trailhead is at 2,490 m, so the mountain costs about a third of the climb it would from the valley.
  • Simple to arrange. A day hike here needs no permit. Park, walk, come back.

Is this for me? Honestly

A long day more than a hard one, until the last {{u:300 m|300 yards}}: a bare rock ridge with a very long drop on one side. Fitness gets you to it. Whether you enjoy it depends on your head for heights.

FitnessIf you can run or cycle for two hours you have the engine. The climbing adds up to two and a half Empire State Buildings, in two instalments going out and a third coming back. What flat-ground fitness does not train is the descent: 300 m of rocky steps near the end, on tired legs.
AltitudeThe whole hike is above 2,480 m and the top is over 3,000 m. Coming from sea level you will be slower and shorter of breath than you expect. Sleep a night up high first if you can (page 4).
The ridgeNo hands needed, no scrambling, and the summit itself is broad. But the crest before it is narrow, with a sheer drop to the north-west and a steep one on the other side. If heights freeze you, stop where the trees end.
Shorter optionLower Sunrise Lake: the same start and the same steep climb, then a lake instead of a summit. 8.6 km there and back (page 3).

Read this first

Stay off the ridge and summit in thunderstorms, or when the rock is wet. That is the Park Service’s own warning for this trailhead. Storms build on summer afternoons, mostly June to September, and the last half hour of this hike is the highest bare rock for a long way around. Be on top before noon. Turn back if you hear thunder, if clouds are towering by late morning, if the rock is wet, or if snow or ice lies on the crest.

Early in the season the hike starts with a wade. Tenaya Creek, two minutes from the car, has been reported thigh-deep in June and waist-deep in a big snow year. This guide was compiled from maps and published reports, not walked. At every junction, choose “Clouds Rest”.

Step by step

{{u:km|miles}} · heightWhat to do, and what to look for
10.0 km2,490 m Sunrise Lakes trailhead. Start from the end of the car park nearest the lake. A path to Olmsted Point leaves to the west almost at once: ignore it and take the boardwalk towards the lake. After two minutes you cross Tenaya Creek, the lake’s outlet, on placed rocks or on foot (see the warning on page 1). Just beyond, at 0.3 km, a trail goes left along the lake shore. Keep right, heading south.
0.3–2.4 kmlevel Easy, shaded walking through lodgepole pine forest, across several small streams that shrink through the summer. Do not let it set your idea of the day.
22.4 km2,515 m The foot of the switchbacks. The trail turns uphill and climbs 290 m in 1.6 km on steps built of rock, some of them broken. This is the hardest work of the hike, so take it slowly; you are also {{u:2,600 m|8,500 ft}} up. The upper bends are shaded and have held snow into mid-June.
33.9 km2,810 m Sunrise Lakes junction, at the top of the climb. Left goes to the Sunrise Lakes (A, {{u:400 m|a quarter mile}} away). For Clouds Rest go straight on, south. The trail now loses 105 m down a slope of granite and open forest. Remember it: you climb this at the end of the day.
45.3 km2,735 m The pond. A small meadow, white boulders with dwarf aspen among them, a still pond beside the trail and a stream or two after it. This is the last water before the summit, 4.5 km and 290 m of climbing away: fill up if you are low (page 4). Then 2 km of nearly level forest.
57.2 km2,775 m Forsyth Trail junction. Keep right. Left drops away to Sunrise Creek and the John Muir Trail, a long way from your car. From here the climb is steady: up to a saddle, along the forested back of the ridge and over one or two false tops, with the trees getting smaller and further apart.
9.5 km2,975 m The trees end and the ridge begins. Decide here, looking at the sky and the rock (page 1). The way to the top follows the crest for {{u:270 m|300 yards}} over blocks and slabs: narrow, with a sheer drop on the right going out and a steep one on the left. Walk it; nobody needs to hurry. Maps show an older trail passing below the summit on the south-east side. Reports call it faint or washed out, so do not count on it as a way round.
69.8 km{{u:3,025 m|9,926 ft}} Clouds Rest. The top widens into a platform of flat slabs with room to sit well back from the edge. The table on page 2 says what you are looking at. A phone signal has been reported here.
315.6 km2,810 m Going back, the same way. Keep left at 5. The 105 m climb from the basin to the Sunrise Lakes junction comes at the worst time; save water and something to eat for it. Then take the switchbacks carefully: tired legs and loose rock are how ankles get turned.
119.5 km2,490 m The car, and the lake. The beach at the west end of Tenaya Lake is a few minutes from the trailhead.

The short option: lower Sunrise Lake A

At 3 turn left instead. In {{u:400 m|a quarter of a mile}}, almost level, the trail reaches the shore of the lowest of the three Sunrise Lakes, in a granite hollow with trees to its edge. From the car that is 4.3 km and 360 m of climbing each way: 3–4 hours in all, with the whole of the steep climb but no exposure and no long afternoon. It also works as a fallback if the sky turns while you are on the switchbacks.

Why the top is a ridge: granite, and an island in the ice

One kind of rock. From the car park to the summit you are on pale, speckled granite. Geologists call the rock of this part of the park the Half Dome Granodiorite: molten rock that cooled slowly, deep underground, about 87 million years ago, and has since been lifted and stripped bare.

Ice up to your neck. In the last ice age, which peaked 15,000–20,000 years ago, an ice field more than 500 m thick covered the high country behind you. Part of it spilled over a low pass into the basin of Tenaya Lake and flowed on down Tenaya Canyon, on your right as you walk the ridge. The geologist François Matthes worked out that only the tops of Clouds Rest and Half Dome stood clear of it, “like bold rocky isles”. The saddle between them was under {{u:210 m|700 ft}} of ice.

That is why the summit is a narrow crest. Everything lower was ground smooth and rounded by moving ice; the last stretch was never covered, and has been left to frost, which splits it into the stacked slabs you walk on.

{{PHOTO:face}}

The big slope. Below the ridge the mountain falls about {{u:1,400 m|4,500 ft}} to Tenaya Creek in one sweep of bare rock, “said to be one of the largest continuous rock slopes in the world”, as a 1960 trail guide put it. Granite formed under pressure cracks in curved sheets, parallel to its own surface, when the weight above it is removed. Here the sheets are hollow in the bowls and bulge on the spurs between, which gives the wall its rippled look from Olmsted Point.

Two names from 1851. Both were given by the Mariposa Battalion, the militia sent to drive the Ahwahneechee people out of Yosemite. A party exploring above the valley turned back when they saw clouds settling on this mountain, and a snowstorm followed: Clouds Rest. The lake they named for Tenaya, the chief they captured there. His own people’s name for it was recorded as Py-we-ack.

When to go

  • The road decides. The trailhead is on Tioga Road, which is closed by snow from about November until late May or June. It opened on 15 May in 2026, 26 May in 2025, 10 June in 2024 and 22 July in 2023, and closed for the winter on 12 November 2026. Recorded road conditions: +1 209 372 0200.
  • July to September is the season for most people. In June expect the wade at the start, snow on the shaded switchbacks that can hide the trail, and mosquitoes in the first flat stretch, worst in the morning.
  • September and early October: no mosquitoes, fewer storms, shorter days, and less water on the trail.
  • Start by 7:00. It puts you on top before the afternoon clouds and finds you a parking space.

Water

  • Carry 3 litres each from the car. There is no drinking water at the trailhead that I could confirm.
  • On the trail: Tenaya Creek and small streams in the first 2.4 km, then the pond and streams in the basin around 4. Nothing on the final 4.5 km, which is the sunniest part.
  • Late in the season the small streams fail. Tenaya Creek at the start is usually dry by late summer, and in October 2026 the park reported only year-round lakes and rivers flowing. I found no dated report on the pond itself, so from September carry all you need.
  • Treat it with a filter or tablets. The streams are clear; water from the pond is still and will have bits in it.

Bring

  • Sun hat, sunscreen and sunglasses: thin air and pale rock.
  • A warm layer and a windproof for the top, even on a hot day.
  • Food for a full day, plus something salty.
  • Shoes with grip. Poles help on the switchbacks coming down.
  • In June: sandals for the creek, and insect repellent.
  • A paper map or the GPX on your phone, and a headtorch in case the day runs long. Expect no signal except near 3 and on the summit.

Rules and fees

  • No permit is needed for a day hike. Camping needs a wilderness permit.
  • Park entry: $35 per car, valid seven days. Since 2026 visitors who are not US residents pay a further $100 per person aged 16 or over, unless they hold an annual pass. No entry reservation was needed in 2026; I found nothing announced for 2027, so check nps.gov/yose.
  • Bears. By day, food may stay in the car if it is out of sight with the windows shut. After dark it must go in the metal food lockers at the trailhead. On the trail keep your pack within arm’s reach.
  • No dogs and no bicycles on the trail. Carry out all rubbish.

The night before

Sleeping high helps with the altitude and the early start. On Tioga Road the park’s Porcupine Flat and Tuolumne Meadows campgrounds opened in late June and July in 2026; all need a reservation, at recreation.gov. Lee Vining has motels.

Getting to the trailhead

{{ACCESS}}

Solid blue: from Yosemite Valley, 75 km (47 miles). Dashes: from Lee Vining and Highway 395, 46 km (29 miles). Basemap: USGS The National Map. Routes: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

Sunrise Lakes trailhead is on Tioga Road (Highway 120) at the western end of Tenaya Lake. The road is paved throughout: any car should be fine. It is slow and winding, so allow 1¼–1½ hours from Yosemite Valley and about 50 minutes from Lee Vining. Fill up first: there is no fuel in Yosemite Valley or at Tuolumne Meadows.

AFrom Yosemite Valley, leave by Northside Drive, following signs for Highway 120.
BAfter 10 km (6½ mi) turn right onto Big Oak Flat Road and climb out of the valley.
C15 km (9½ mi) on, at Crane Flat, turn right onto Tioga Road. Fuel here, by card, day and night. Arriving by Highway 120 from the west, Crane Flat is 12 km (7½ mi) past the Big Oak Flat entrance, and Tioga Road is a left turn.
D47 km (29 mi) along Tioga Road, Olmsted Point. Stop for five minutes: it has the best view of the mountain you are about to climb.
P2.4 km (1½ mi) further, as the road comes down to Tenaya Lake, the trailhead car park is on the right.
LFrom the east: from Lee Vining on Highway 395 take Highway 120 west for 19 km (12 mi) up to the E Tioga Pass entrance, then 25 km (16 mi) through Tuolumne Meadows and along Tenaya Lake. The car park is on the left at the far end of the lake.

Parking

Free with park entry. The car park is small, about a dozen spaces by one account, and fills early; after that people park beside the road nearby. Keep out of the signed no-parking stretch opposite. Where you park on the roadside, obey the signs. Vault toilets and food lockers at the trailhead. No overnight parking on Tioga Road from 15 October.

Without a car

Difficult. A park shuttle between Olmsted Point and Tuolumne Meadows stopped at this trailhead in summer 2026, until 13 September, but the park says it does not run every year. The hikers’ bus from Yosemite Valley reaches Tenaya Lake at about 10:10 and leaves at 2:30 pm, which is not long enough. YARTS buses on this road do not list a stop at Tenaya Lake.

Afterwards

  • Going east: Whoa Nellie Deli, inside the petrol station where Highway 120 meets 395 at Lee Vining. A local institution, open roughly late April to early October.
  • Nearest: the Tuolumne Meadows Grill, 15 minutes east. Breakfast and lunch only, so better before the hike than after. Open 30 May to 20 September in 2026.
  • Going west: Rush Creek Lodge, just outside the Big Oak Flat entrance, has a tavern serving lunch and dinner. In Groveland, 40 minutes further, the Iron Door Saloon grill is open until 8 pm, 9 pm on Friday and Saturday.
