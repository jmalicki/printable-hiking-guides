One of the Bay Area’s most strenuous day hikes · Ohlone Regional Wilderness, above Livermore

Chasing the rain to Murietta Falls

Go within days of a winter storm and a stream you could step across slides {{u:thirty metres|a hundred feet}} down a rock face, {{u:ten kilometres|six miles}} from the nearest road. To reach it you climb one ridge, drop off the back of it and climb a higher one. Then you do all of it again to get home.

{{PHOTO:falls}}

Why this hike

Most waterfalls near San Francisco Bay are a short stroll from a car park, and you share them. This one sits in the middle of {{u:3,940 hectares|9,737 acres}} of hills that no public road enters: the park district’s own description is that the wilderness can be reached only on foot or on horseback. From Del Valle, the eastern end of the Ohlone Wilderness Trail, the falls make one very full day.

  • A waterfall you have to time. It runs hard for a few days after each storm. Catching it is a small triumph; page 2 tells you how to improve the odds.
  • The hills are the point. From January to April the ridges are green to the skyline, with oaks standing alone in the grass, Lake Del Valle shrinking behind you and Mount Diablo to the north. The hike is worth the day when the falls are a damp streak.
  • Real quiet. No road, no buildings, and by every report few people. An hour’s drive from several million of them, that is rare.
  • No paperwork. The trail permit that this hike used to need was abolished on 1 January 2026. You pay for parking and go.

Is this for me?

Only if you have already done a full day in steep hills. Being able to run for an hour on the flat does not prepare your legs for {{u:1,300 m|4,200 ft}} of descent.

Overall effortA full day, and among the most strenuous day hikes around San Francisco Bay: one local guide likens it to climbing Mount Diablo, but far more remote. The climb equals a 400-storey building.
The shapeUp 490 m to Rocky Ridge, down 150 m into Williams Gulch, up 460 m to the high point, down 115 m to the falls. Going home reverses them all.
SteepnessThe two big climbs average 16–17% for {{u:two kilometres|well over a mile}} each, with steeper stretches. Nothing needs hands.

Bail-outIf the climb to Rocky Ridge (point 3) felt hard, turn round there. The second climb is longer.

Read this first

Del Valle’s gate is locked in the evening, and cars left inside may be cited or towed. In midwinter the park may be open for only nine hours, for a hike that takes seven to nine (page 5). Be at the gate when it opens, and turn round when half your open hours are gone, wherever you are: the way back takes almost as long as the way out.

Will there be water?

The falls follow the last storm. Of twelve dated first-hand reports from 2002 to 2021:

  • Six found it running hard, all between January and March, and where the report says, within days of heavy rain.
  • Two found a modest flow (March and April 2014, a drought year, a week after storms).
  • Four found a trickle: January 2006, June 2006, May 2016 and March 2021.

The district’s own newsletter promises “a rushing fall, a thread-like trickle, or no water at all”.

So: go between late December and March, two to five days after a storm that dropped real rain on Livermore. In a dry winter, go for the hills.

Field notes

Why these hills look the way they do

Cattle have grazed the Ohlone Wilderness for about a century and still do. Its wildness is emptiness. What you walk through is decided by three things: two kinds of rock that meet in Williams Gulch, a climate with no summer rain, and which way each slope faces.

{{PHOTO:oak}}
1 · Sandstone and oak savannathe first climb, points 1–3

Rocky Ridge is built of sandstone, siltstone and pebbly conglomerate laid down on a sea floor in the late Cretaceous, part of what geologists call the Great Valley sequence. It carries grass and scattered oaks, each tree standing alone: the park district lists coast live oak, valley oak and blue oak here, with madrone and the pale, long-needled grey pine. Oaks spaced out in grass like parkland are the signature of California’s dry inner hills, and you will be looking at them all day. Reports of this climb also mention toyon, with red berries in winter, and manzanita.

{{PHOTO:gulch}}
2 · The gulchWilliams Gulch, point 4

The creek has cut down along the line where the two rock types meet; the 1996 US Geological Survey map shows a sliver of serpentinite, the slippery green rock of California’s fault zones, at about the crossing. The bottom of the fold is out of the sun for most of the day and holds water into summer, so in a hundred paces you go from open grass to a wood of California bay laurel (crush a leaf: sharp, peppery) and oak, with poison oak beneath. The district lists sycamore, alder and willow along the creeks of these hills. California newts live here too: slow, orange-bellied, out on wet days, and poisonous to eat.

{{PHOTO:rock}}
3 · The jumbled ridgeBig Burn to the falls, points 4–8

Across the creek the rock is the Franciscan mélange: sea-floor mud and sandstone that were scraped off a sinking ocean plate and kneaded together, with hard blocks of chert, greenstone and schist floating in the sheared mass like fruit in a cake. The soft matrix makes rounded, slumping grassland; the hard blocks stand out as crags. Schlieper Rock is one such outcrop. The north-facing wall of the gulch, where the Big Burn climbs, carries woodland and brush; the sunny ridge top above is almost all grass. In August 2020 the SCU Lightning Complex fire burned about {{u:1,800 hectares|4,500 acres}} of the wilderness, and you may still see its traces.

{{PHOTO:pond}}
4 · Grass, cattle and pondsthe ridge tops, points 5–7

Livermore gets about {{u:390 mm|15 inches}} of rain a year, nearly all of it from November to March, and on average none in July. The grass follows that clock exactly: green from the first rains, knee-high and full of poppies, lupins and goldfields in April, dead and gold by June. Like most California grassland, it is now largely annual grasses that arrived from Europe with livestock. Johnny’s Pond is a stock pond, said to be named for John Fernandes, a ranch hand here. The park district keeps cattle on the land to hold down the dry grass that carries fire; whether grazing helps or harms these hills is argued over. Give cattle room: walk round a group and stay well clear of calves.

Why the falls come and go. Look at the profile on page 2: the falls are only about 115 m below the highest ground on the route. The basin that feeds them is a few gentle hollows of grassland near the ridge top. There is no snowpack and little deep soil to store water, so the creek rises within hours of rain and falls away within weeks. One long-running waterfall log puts the useful life of a storm at about forty days, and a trickle by mid-May even after a wet spring. The height usually quoted is {{u:30 m|100 ft}}, and the falls are often called the tallest in the Bay Area. Neither is a surveyed figure; one careful observer puts the whole cascade at {{u:24 m|80 ft}}, with a largest single drop of {{u:8 m|25 ft}}.

Who lives here. The district lists golden eagles, bobcats, mountain lions, deer and a herd of tule elk; a hiker here in February 2011 watched a golden eagle from the trail. The people of these hills were Ohlone, speakers of the Chochenyo language, and bedrock mortars for grinding acorns survive in the neighbouring Sunol valley.

The name. Joaquín Murrieta was the Gold Rush outlaw of the 1850s, half history and half legend, whose gang is tied by tradition to the Diablo Range; one local history says only that he “perhaps” crossed these hills. The falls are commonly said to be named for him. The district’s map spells it “Murietta”, which is not how the man’s name is usually written.

Getting to Del Valle

{{ACCESS}}

Driving route from Interstate 580: 18.4 km (11.4 miles), about 30 minutes. Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

Del Valle Regional Park, 7000 Del Valle Road, Livermore, CA 94550. Livermore is about an hour east of San Francisco without traffic. The road in is paved and winds over one ridge: any car should be fine.

ALeave I-580 at North Livermore Avenue and drive south.
BGo straight through the middle of Livermore. The road becomes South Livermore Avenue and then, as it leaves town through vineyards, Tesla Road.
C5.8 km (3.6 mi) from the freeway, turn right onto Mines Road.
DAfter 5.6 km (3.5 mi) Mines Road turns away to the left. Keep straight on: this is Del Valle Road, which climbs over a ridge and drops to the lake in 6.3 km (3.9 mi). Pay at the entrance kiosk on the way down.
PAt the bottom, cross the bridge and turn right at the T-junction. Drive {{u:500 m|a third of a mile}} to the far end of the car parks: Lichen Bark picnic area. The gated dirt road at the end is the trail.

Parking, fees and the gate

Parking is $5 per vehicle ($10 at weekends and on holidays from April to early September), paid at the kiosk in cash or by card. No reservation and, since January 2026, no trail permit.

Gate hours are the catch. The district’s Del Valle page gives 7 am–6 pm from November to February and 6 am–7 pm in March. Its Ohlone page gives 8 am–5 pm until the end of January and 8 am–6 pm in February. I could not settle which is right: ask at the kiosk, and until then assume the shorter day. That is nine hours in midwinter for a hike of seven to nine. February and March give you more room.

Practical

  • When: late December to March for the falls; January to April for green hills and flowers. Avoid May to October: Livermore averages {{u:31–32 °C|88–89 °F}} in July and August, and the falls are dry.
  • Check before you go: ebparks.org/alerts-closures. In November 2026 nothing on this route was closed.
  • Bring: shoes with a deep tread, poles if you own them (for the descents and the creek), a warm layer and a waterproof, a headtorch, sun protection, a paper map, and more food than you think. Winter reports describe thick, sticky mud on the ranch roads: it costs time.
  • Rules: dogs by day only, on a lead near cattle; no bicycles on this trail; no smoking, no drones, no fires. Close every gate. Stay on the trail: some of the land beside it is closed.
  • Staying out: Stewart’s Camp is {{u:750 m|half a mile}} beyond the falls. Reserve at least two working days ahead: +1 888 327 2757, option 2.

Water and hazards

  • Carry all your water: 3 litres each in cool weather. The district map shows drinking water at the car park, and its camps have spring water that must be filtered or boiled and “may not be available”. Fill up at home.
  • Ticks: hikers report them in winter and spring; check yourself afterwards. Poison oak (leaves in threes, bare in winter and still harmful) lines Williams Gulch and the Big Burn. The district warns of rattlesnakes.
  • You are a long way from help. Tell someone your route and return time. Emergency: 911, or the district’s 24-hour dispatch, +1 510 881 1121.
