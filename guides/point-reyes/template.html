A day hike · Point Reyes National Seashore, an hour north of San Francisco

Through the firs above Drakes Bay

Follow a creek through bay laurel and alder until the trees stop at the edge of the Pacific, then climb home along the crest of the peninsula under tall Douglas firs, with Drakes Bay opening behind you. It is {{u:18 kilometres|11 miles}}, and nearly all the climbing comes in the second half.

{{PHOTO:hero}}

Why this hike

Most people who start at Bear Valley walk the valley trail to the coast and come back the way they went: {{u:6.5 km|four miles}} of level forest each way, to an overlook that has been closed since 2015. This loop uses the valley as the way out and comes home over the ridge above it, so the second half of the day is new ground.

  • Two forests. Bay laurel and alder along the creek in the morning; Douglas fir and sword fern on the ridge in the afternoon.
  • The ocean in the middle. The valley ends on a bluff above the Pacific, and the trail follows the bluff for {{u:800 m|half a mile}}.
  • The top of the peninsula. The Sky Trail runs the crest of Inverness Ridge for {{u:6 km|four miles}} to Mount Wittenberg, the highest point in the park.
  • Free and simple. No entrance fee, no permit, a large car park, and a signpost at every junction.

Is this for me?

Yes, if you can stay on your feet for five or six hours. Nothing here is steep for long or rough underfoot; the day is made of distance and one long, steady climb.

Overall effortLike a brisk {{u:23 km|14-mile}} walk on the flat. Someone who can jog {{u:5 km|5 km}} can do it at an easy pace with a long lunch.
The shape{{u:7.4 km|4.6 miles}} of almost level valley to the sea. Then 360 m of climbing spread over 6 km on the Sky Trail, a last 80 m to the summit, and 385 m down in {{u:3.4 km|two miles}}.

Practical

  • When: any month. March to June for long days and wildflowers on the bluff. September and October are the clearest months on this coast. Summer is often foggy and cool by the sea.
  • Check before you go: trail conditions at nps.gov/pore, or phone the visitor centre on +1 415 464 5100. When this guide was compiled the park listed every trail on the loop as open, with fallen trees to step round or duck under on the Bear Valley Trail beyond point 3 and on the Mount Wittenberg Trail.
  • Water: fill up at the trailhead tap and carry 2 litres for the loop.
  • Bring: a windproof layer even on a hot day inland, sun protection for the bluff, lunch, and long trousers: the Coast and Sky trails are narrow and grow in between mowings.
  • Rules: no dogs on any of these trails. Bicycles only as far as point 3. No drones.

Why this way round

The valley is the easy half, so it comes first. Going this way you climb the Sky Trail with the sea behind you and come down the steep Mount Wittenberg Trail, which is {{u:2.8 km|1.7 miles}} of unbroken hill: better as a descent at the end than as a climb at the start.

Hazards

  • Poison oak lines the Coast and Sky trails: leaves in threes, shiny, red in autumn, bare and still harmful in winter. Stinging nettle grows in the damp gullies.
  • Ticks wait on grass and brush beside the trail, and some carry Lyme disease. Check yourself at the end of the day.
  • The bluff edge at the coast is unstable and crumbles. Stay on the trail and out of the closed ground at Arch Rock.
  • Burned trees on the ridge can drop limbs or fall in wind. Do not stop under them on a windy day.
  • Mountain lions live here and are very rarely seen. Do not run from one; stand tall and back away.
  • Emergency: 911, or park dispatch on +1 415 464 5170.

On the April 2026 timetable the first bus arrives at 8:52 am on weekdays and 8:35 at weekends, and the last one back leaves at 8:36 pm (8:24 at weekends), which leaves time for this loop in any season. Bear Valley Visitor Center, 1 Bear Valley Road, Point Reyes Station, CA 94956. About {{u:50 km|30 miles}} and an hour and a quarter from San Francisco. The road is paved all the way and winding for the second half.

Parking and fees

Free. Point Reyes charges no entrance fee and day hikers need no permit. The car park holds about 260 cars, with toilets and drinking water beside it.

Field notes

A peninsula from somewhere else

The car park and the summit of Mount Wittenberg stand on different pieces of the Earth’s crust from the village of Olema, {{u:a kilometre|half a mile}} to the east. That one fact explains most of what you see on this hike.

{{PHOTO:divide}}
1 · The fault valleyBear Valley, points 1–3

The San Andreas Fault runs down the Olema Valley, past the trailhead. Everything west of it rides on the Pacific Plate, which is sliding north-west past the rest of California at {{u:3.5–5 cm|about an inch and a half}} a year. The granite under Inverness Ridge is 80 to 100 million years old and matches rock in the mountains near Tehachapi, some {{u:500 km|300 miles}} to the south-east, which is where the park’s geologists think it started. In the earthquake of 1906 the ground near here shifted by as much as {{u:7.5 m|24 feet}} in under a minute. The Earthquake Trail, a {{u:1 km|0.6 mile}} paved loop from the picnic area beside the car park, shows a fence line that was pulled apart.

{{PHOTO:coast}}
2 · The edge of the landCoast Trail, points 4–5

Out of the valley and into the wind, the trees give way to coastal scrub: coyote brush and lupin, with poison oak among them. The bluffs are soft layered rock, and the sea cuts them back all the time. Arch Rock stood at the mouth of this valley until 21 March 2015, when part of the overlook collapsed, killing one walker and injuring another. The park has kept the spur trail and the ground round it closed ever since, and warns that the land may still be moving. Winter storms have also taken the bottom of the old steps to Kelham Beach, a little further up the coast.

{{PHOTO:sky}}
3 · The fir ridgeSky Trail and Mount Wittenberg, points 5–1

Inverness Ridge is the granite backbone of the peninsula, and it stands in the path of the summer fog. Fog caught in the needles drips to the ground through the rainless months, and it feeds a forest of Douglas fir, the big tree of the Pacific coast from here to Canada, with sword fern to the knee under it. The valley trail at the start runs under bay laurel, whose leaves smell sharply of pepper when crushed, with alders along the creek.

{{PHOTO:burn}}
4 · The 2020 fireSky and Mount Wittenberg trails, points 5–1

On 17 and 18 August 2020 lightning started a fire near the coast, north of where you join the Sky Trail. The Woodward Fire burned {{u:1,995 hectares|4,929 acres}} before it was contained on 1 October, and the last trail closures were lifted two years later. Along the Sky and Mount Wittenberg trails it mostly cleared the undergrowth and left the big trees alive, so the forest is more open than it was, with charred bark at the foot of green firs. The park still warns that dead trees may fall in wind.

{{PHOTO:quail}}
5 · Who lives hereall day

Black-tailed deer are the animal you are surest to see, in Divide Meadow and at the edge of the forest. California quail, the bird naturalists have logged most often along this loop, run across the trail in coveys, each with a black plume nodding over its forehead. Sonoma chipmunks and acorn woodpeckers are both common in the records. Among the mammals recorded here, bobcats come third, ahead of grey foxes and coyotes: watch the edges of meadows in the first and last hours of light. The dark blue jay of the fir forest is the Steller’s jay. From the bluff, grey whales pass southbound in December and January and northbound, with calves, around late March.
