A very long day hike · Point Reyes National Seashore, an hour north of San Francisco

The long way to Alamere Falls

Follow a forest valley down to the Pacific, walk the sand at low tide to a waterfall that lands on the beach, then climb home over the highest ground on the peninsula. It is {{u:31 kilometres|19 miles}}, and the tide table sets your alarm clock.

{{PHOTO:hero}}

Why this hike

Alamere Falls is the best-known sight on this coast: a creek that runs off the edge of a {{u:12 m|40 ft}} cliff straight onto the beach. The short way in ends on an unmaintained path down that crumbling cliff, which the park asks people to stay off. This hike arrives the way the park recommends, along the sand from Wildcat Camp, and turns the trip into a tour of the whole peninsula.

  • You meet the falls from below, after {{u:1.8 km|a mile}} of empty beach under tall bluffs, with the surf beside you.
  • Four kinds of country in a day: a shaded creek valley, a fir forest, open bluffs above the sea, and a ridge crest.
  • A loop with almost no repeats. Only the beach and one hill are walked twice.
  • Free and simple. No entrance fee, no permit, a large car park, and drinking water half-way round.

Is this for me?

If you have been on your feet for eight hours before and finished well, yes. Nothing here is steep or rough. The difficulty is the distance, and a clock run by the tide and the sunset.

Overall effortThree-quarters of a marathon with the climb of a 300-storey building. Someone who can jog {{u:5 km|5 km}} can do it, slowly, given a full day.
The shapeA gentle valley, then up 170 m and down 220 m to the sea. A flat beach. Then rolling bluffs and one long climb of 380 m that starts at {{u:kilometre 21|mile 13}}.

Read this first

The beach to the falls can only be walked around low tide. At higher tides the sea covers it and waves reach the foot of the bluffs. Look up the low tide before you choose a day (page 2), and if waves are touching the bluffs when you get there, leave the falls out.

Stay off the cliffs and away from their base. They are soft and shed rock; the park carries out several rescues here every year. In December and January there are under ten hours of daylight: take the short cut, or come back in spring.

Timing the tide

Wildcat Camp is {{u:10 km|six miles}} from the car, 2½ to 3 hours at a steady pace. The beach to the falls and back takes an hour or more with time to look.

  • Leave the car about 3½ hours before low tide, so that you are on the sand while the water is still going out.
  • Tide times: the US government’s predictions for station 9415020, Point Reyes, at tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov. Print the day’s table and bring it.
  • The park will not name a tide height that makes the beach safe: storms move the sand, and swell matters as much as the tide. A lower low tide is better. Judge it with your eyes at Wildcat.
  • A low tide between about 9 am and 1 pm fits the whole loop into a spring or summer day. An afternoon low tide means walking the loop the other way round, with the mountain first.

Never turn your back on the water. The park warns of sneaker waves and rip currents here, and the sea is about {{u:10 °C|50 °F}}.

The people on the ledges above came down the cliff path; leave that to them. Watch the time and the water, and start back while the tide is still low.

It narrows to a footpath through coastal scrub, high above the sea, dipping through gullies. Wildcat Beach and the cliffs of Double Point lie behind you; ahead the coast curves away to the Point Reyes headland. In winter and spring, look for the spouts of grey whales. Field notes

A peninsula from somewhere else

The car park and the summit of Mount Wittenberg stand on different pieces of the Earth’s crust from the village of Olema, {{u:a kilometre|half a mile}} to the east. That one fact explains most of what you see on this hike.

{{PHOTO:divide}}
1 · The fault valleyBear Valley, points 1–3

The San Andreas Fault runs down the Olema Valley, past the trailhead. Everything west of it rides on the Pacific Plate, which is sliding north-west past the rest of California at {{u:3.5–5 cm|about an inch and a half}} a year. The granite under Inverness Ridge is 80 to 100 million years old and matches rock in the mountains near Tehachapi, some {{u:500 km|300 miles}} to the south-east, which is where the park’s geologists think it started. In the earthquake of 1906 the ground near here shifted by as much as {{u:7.5 m|24 feet}} in under a minute. The Earthquake Trail, a {{u:1 km|0.6 mile}} paved loop from the picnic area beside the car park, shows a fence line that was pulled apart.

{{PHOTO:sky}}
2 · The fir ridgeSky Trail and Mount Wittenberg, points 8–1

Inverness Ridge is the granite backbone of the peninsula, and it stands in the path of the summer fog. Fog caught in the needles drips to the ground through the rainless months, and it feeds a forest of Douglas fir, the big tree of the Pacific coast from here to Canada, with sword fern to the knee under it. The valley trail at the start runs under bay laurel, whose leaves smell sharply of pepper when crushed, with alders along the creek.

{{PHOTO:burn}}
3 · The 2020 fireSky and Mount Wittenberg trails, points 8–1

On 17 and 18 August 2020 lightning started a fire near the coast, north of where you join the Sky Trail. The Woodward Fire burned {{u:1,995 hectares|4,929 acres}} before it was contained on 1 October, and the last trail closures were lifted two years later. Along the Sky and Mount Wittenberg trails it mostly cleared the undergrowth and left the big trees alive, so the forest is more open than it was, with charred bark at the foot of green firs. The park still warns that dead trees may fall in wind.

{{PHOTO:falls}}
4 · Bluffs, sand and the fallsWildcat Beach and the Coast Trail, points 5–8

Out of the fog-drip and into the wind, the trees give way to coastal scrub: coyote brush and lupin, with poison oak among them. The bluffs are soft layered rock that the sea cuts back faster than the creeks can cut down, and that is why Alamere Creek ends in mid-air. The park gives the drop as about {{u:12 m|40 ft}}; other descriptions run from {{u:9 to 15 m|30 to 50 ft}}. Above the lip, out of sight from the beach, the creek steps down three smaller falls. The same retreating cliffs took Arch Rock in March 2015, when the arch fell with walkers on top of it and one of them died.

{{PHOTO:elk}}
5 · Who lives hereall day

Tule elk, a small pale elk found only in California, were released in the wilderness south of Limantour Beach in 1998; the park counted 199 in that free-ranging herd in 2023, and a camper in June 2023 had them grazing through Wildcat Camp all evening. Give them {{u:50 metres|fifty yards}}, more in the autumn rut. Black-tailed deer are the animal you are surest to see, in Divide Meadow and on the bluffs. California quail run across the trail in coveys, each with a black plume nodding over its forehead, and Sonoma chipmunks scold from the scrub. Among the records naturalists have logged along this route, bobcats come third among the mammals, ahead of coyotes and grey foxes: watch the edges of meadows in the first and last hours of light. From the Coast Trail, grey whales pass southbound in December and January and northbound, with calves, around late March.

Hazards

  • Poison oak lines the Glen, Coast and Sky trails: leaves in threes, shiny, red in autumn, bare and still harmful in winter. Stinging nettle grows in the damp gullies.
  • Ticks wait on grass and brush beside the trail, and some carry Lyme disease. Check yourself at the end of the day.
  • Mountain lions live here and are very rarely seen. Do not run from one; stand tall and back away.
  • Emergency: 911, or park dispatch on +1 415 464 5170.

Afterwards

By bus

Marin Transit route 68 runs daily from the San Rafael Transit Center to the visitor centre. On the April 2026 timetable the first bus arrives at 8:52 am on weekdays and 8:35 at weekends, and the last one back leaves at 8:36 pm (8:24 at weekends), which fits this hike from April to August. Check the times at marintransit.gov/routes/68.

Bear Valley Visitor Center, 1 Bear Valley Road, Point Reyes Station, CA 94956. About {{u:50 km|30 miles}} and an hour and a quarter from San Francisco. The road is paved all the way and winding for the second half.

Parking and fees

Free. Point Reyes charges no entrance fee and day hikers need no permit. The car park holds about 260 cars, with toilets and drinking water beside it.

Practical

  • When: March to June for a full waterfall, long days, wildflowers on the bluffs and whales offshore. September and October are the clearest months on this coast. Summer is often foggy and cool by the sea.
  • Daylight: sunrise to sunset is 9½ hours on 21 December (7:23 am–4:55 pm) and almost 15 hours on 21 June (5:48 am–8:37 pm).
  • Check before you go: trail conditions at nps.gov/pore, or phone the visitor centre on +1 415 464 5100. In November 2026 the park listed no closure on this loop.
  • Water: taps at the trailhead and at Wildcat Camp. Carry 2 litres and refill at Wildcat.
  • Bring: the tide table, a windproof layer even on a hot day inland, sun protection, a headtorch, a full day’s food, and long trousers: the Coast and Sky trails grow in between mowings, and a June 2023 report found the brush on the Coast Trail at face height.
  • Rules: no dogs on any of these trails. Bicycles only as far as point 3. No drones.
