A day hike · Castle Rock State Park, on the Skyline ridge above Saratoga

The cliff path at Castle Rock

Walk round a sandstone crag eaten into caves and honeycomb, stand over the lip of a waterfall, then follow a trail cut into an open mountainside with a forested valley under your left hand. You come back through oak and madrone woods. One short ledge on the way out needs a steady head.

Why this hike

Many visitors stroll to Castle Rock, look at the waterfall, and drive home. The park’s best ground starts where they turn round: a path along the open western face of the ridge, with nothing but forest between you and Monterey Bay.

  • Rock you can put your hand inside. The sandstone weathers into pockets, ribs and small caves, at Castle Rock, Goat Rock and all along the loop.
  • A waterfall seen from above, from a platform over the lip of the 23 m (75 ft) Castle Rock Falls.
  • {{u:Two kilometres|Over a mile}} in the open. Rare in these wooded mountains: chaparral, bare rock and a view the whole way.

Is this for me?

Short, and rougher underfoot than its length suggests. The question is the ledge: read the box below first.

Read this first: the cabled ledge

About 1 km beyond the Interconnector junction, at 6 on the map, the Saratoga Gap Trail crosses a steep rock face on a narrow ledge. A steel cable bolted to the rock is the handrail, and the slope falls away sharply below. It is short, and day hikers cross it all year, but a slip there would be serious. The park’s brochure and hiking page do not mention it, and it is on the signed way to the trail camp.

Go another way if heights unsettle you, you have young children with you, you are carrying a heavy overnight pack, or the rock is wet or icy. The Ridge Trail avoids it: take the short loop from 5, or go to the trail camp and back by the Ridge Trail (page 3).

Why this way round

Castle Rock comes first, before the climbers and families arrive. Going out on the Saratoga Gap Trail puts the view in front of you and brings you to the ledge fresh, in the first half of the day, with the choice to turn back at 5 still open. The Ridge Trail, shaded and without exposure, takes the climb home.

When to go

All year. The falls run best from December to April and can shrink to a trickle by late summer. The open hillside is hot on summer afternoons, so start early then. After rain the rock is slick and the ledge is at its worst; the park also closes the crags to climbers for up to three days.

Junctions are signed with trail names and distances. Two trails make the loop: the Saratoga Gap Trail out, the Ridge Trail back.

53.9 km799 m Interconnector Trail. The decision point. To miss the ledge, turn right here (see below). Otherwise keep left on the Saratoga Gap Trail. It squeezes through a gap between boulders, where you may have to turn sideways, and returns to the open slope, rockier now.

Keep a hand on the cable and let people coming the other way clear the ledge before you start. Hikers describe the rock as grippy even when damp, and the passage as shorter and easier than it looks. This is also the lowest point of the day.
75.4 km723 m Ridge Trail, far end. The path swings right into forest and meets the Ridge Trail. The trail camp is {{u:270 m|0.2 mi}} further on the Saratoga Gap Trail, across a small creek. You may hear gunfire here from a private shooting range beyond the park boundary.
85.7 km741 m Castle Rock Trail Camp. Picnic tables and vault toilets under tall trees: the place for lunch. Return to 7 and take the Ridge Trail, which climbs steadily through madrones, their bark peeling in red curls, and black oaks.

Shorter, and no ledge · 6.7 km, 350 m of climbing

At 5 turn right on the Interconnector Trail (blue dashes), which climbs {{u:250 m|0.15 mi}} to the Ridge Trail at 9. Turn right again for Goat Rock. You keep Castle Rock, the falls, the first open hillside and Goat Rock, and take about 2½ hours.

To the trail camp without the ledge · 4.2 km each way

Go out and back by the Ridge Trail: from 1 down the Saratoga Gap Trail to 3, right over Goat Rock to 7, then left to the camp. The outward leg mostly descends; the return climbs 320 m. This is the way to carry an overnight pack.

Field notes

Sandstone full of holes

Sea-floor sand. The crags are Vaqueros sandstone: coarse sand that settled on the continental shelf some 30 to 40 million years ago, hardened, and was carried north and lifted by movement along the San Andreas Fault, which runs along the eastern foot of this ridge.

How the holes form. The pits, honeycombs and caves are called tafoni. In the wet winters, slightly acid rainwater soaks into the rock and dissolves the mineral cement between the sand grains. In the long dry summers that water is drawn back to the surface and evaporates, leaving the minerals there. The outside of the rock grows a hard crust while the inside weakens. Where the crust breaks, the loose sand behind it falls out and a pocket opens; once open, it grows faster than the surface around it.

Climbers. People have climbed Castle Rock and Goat Rock for nearly a century, and the boulders round them are a well-known bouldering ground. You will see chalked holds, crash pads and ropes. Damp sandstone is fragile, so the park closes the rocks to climbing after rain.

{{PHOTO:rock2}}

Trees out of place. California black oaks, rare elsewhere in these mountains, grow along the ridge, and a few stunted redwoods stand above 760 m (2,500 ft) on the Saratoga Gap Trail, near the upper limit for the species and far smaller than their kin in the canyons.

Who lives here. Acorn woodpeckers work the oaks, loud and easy to see. Peregrine falcons live in the park and hunt along the cliffs; turkey vultures and red-tailed hawks ride the updraught below the trail. Black-tailed deer, grey foxes, coyotes and California newts are all recorded, and mountain lions are present and very rarely seen. The marbled murrelet, a threatened seabird, nests in the park’s old conifers, which is why the signs ask you to leave no crumbs: scraps draw the jays that raid its nests.

How it became a park

In 1908, while the hillsides round it were still being logged, Judge Joseph Welch bought 60 acres on this ridge and opened them to the public. Castle Rock became a popular outing. Russell Varian, a physicist who had explored these canyons as a boy, took an option on the land in 1959, meaning to give it to the state, and died before the sale was complete. The Sierra Club and Sempervirens Fund later gave the land in his memory, and it became a state park in 1968. Varian Peak, above the Saratoga Gap Trail, carries his name.

Practical

  • Hours and fee: open 6 am to sunset. Parking is $10 a car, paid at the entrance station (staffed 8 am to 5 pm) or the card machine.
  • Water and toilets: flush toilets and drinking water at the Kirkwood entrance; vault toilets at the old car park and the trail camp. Carry 1.5 litres each, more in summer.
  • No phone signal in the park or on the road near it. Tell someone your plan.
  • No dogs on any trail. No bikes on these trails, no drones, no smoking.
  • Shoes: something with a grippy sole. The rock is rough but the path over it is worn smooth in places.
  • Watch for poison oak (glossy leaves in threes), thick beside the Ridge Trail, and ticks; check yourself afterwards. Rattlesnakes sun on the open rock in warm weather.
  • Stay on the trail at the falls and on the open hillside: the drops are real, and the slopes below belong to a nature preserve closed to off-trail travel.
  • Emergency: 911. With no signal, the nearest help is the entrance station. Park office: +1 408 868 9540.

Getting to the Kirkwood entrance

Driving route from Saratoga village: 15.3 km. Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

On Skyline Boulevard (Highway 35), {{u:4 km|2.5 mi}} south of Highway 9. About 25 minutes from Saratoga, an hour and a quarter from San Francisco. Paved all the way, and winding: any car should be fine.

AFrom Saratoga village take Highway 9 (Big Basin Way, then Congress Springs Road) west and climb 11.4 km (7.1 mi) of bends to the ridge.
BAt Saratoga Gap, the crossroads on the crest, turn sharp left onto Skyline Boulevard (Highway 35), heading south.
PAfter 3.8 km (2.4 mi) the Robert C. Kirkwood entrance is on the right: a large paved car park, $10. The old dirt car park, {{u:400 m|a quarter of a mile}} further on, is now kept for registered campers. Free parking on the shoulder of Skyline near the entrances is used when the car park is full; obey the signs.

From the coast, Highway 9 climbs to Saratoga Gap from Boulder Creek and Santa Cruz; turn right onto Skyline.

Skyline is popular with cyclists and motorcyclists {{u:at|on}} weekends. Expect both on the bends.

Staying the night

Castle Rock Trail Camp has 20 sites, each with a table and a fire ring, at $15 a site a night for up to six people, plus an $8 reservation fee. Reserve through the Santa Cruz Mountains backcountry trail camp page of California State Parks. Five sites are kept for people who turn up on the day; they must register at the Kirkwood entrance before hiking in. Overnight cars go in the old car park, behind a locked gate. The park’s trail-camp rules say to bring your own water. Fires are allowed in the rings in the rainy season only, with firewood sold at the camp. With an overnight pack, hike in by the Ridge Trail (page 3).
