A day hike and a harbour evening · Montaña de Oro State Park and Morro Bay, central California coast

A summit above the otter harbour

Walk the edge of a wave-cut shelf above sea stacks and tide pools, climb the pyramid behind it to see the whole coast at once, then drive twenty-five minutes to a fishing harbour where sea otters float beside the pier. Dinner is a few steps from the otters. The climb comes in the second half of the hike, in full sun.

Why this hike

Most visitors to Montaña de Oro stroll the Bluff Trail out and back. Most visitors to Morro Bay photograph the Rock and eat. This day joins the two: one loop over shore and summit, then an evening at the harbour you looked down on from the top.

  • The bluffs. {{u:Three kilometres|Two miles}} along a level shelf {{u:12 m|40 ft}} above the surf, past coves, a rock island pierced by tunnels, and tilted beds of shale running out to sea.
  • The summit. Valencia Peak stands directly behind the bluffs. From the top the coast runs from Point Buchon to Morro Rock, with the bay and its sand spit laid out to the north.
  • The otters. Morro Bay harbour has sea otters all year, a few steps from the {{u:pavement|sidewalk}}: mothers with pups beside a pier, rafts asleep off Morro Rock (page 4).
  • Dinner on the water. The restaurants of the Embarcadero start beside the otter pier (page 6).

Is this for me?

Yes, if you can walk for four hours and climb steadily for one of them. The first third is level and wide; the summit is a real climb with no shade.

Overall effortLike a brisk {{u:16 km|10-mile}} walk on the flat. A gentle 120 m up from the shore to the foot of the peak, then 245 m in the last 1.7 km: 45 minutes of continuous uphill.
UnderfootThe Bluff Trail is wide, level gravel, good for a wheelchair. After 5 it is narrow dirt path. Trainers with grip are enough.
Sun and windOpen scrub and grass all the way. In summer the coast is often {{u:15 °C or more|30 °F}} cooler than inland.
EdgesThe bluffs are soft rock, and storms undercut them: the path down to Corallina Cove is closed for that reason. Stay on the trail, back from the rim.
ShorterThe bluffs alone, as a loop: 6.0 km, 1½–2 hours (blue dashes on the map). Or the peak alone: 7.0 km there and back.
BringBinoculars, for both halves of the day. 2 litres of water, a sun hat and a windproof layer.

Why this way round

The bluffs come first because they are level, and because fog is common here on summer mornings and often lifts by midday, when you reach the top. You walk the shore with the sea on your right, and you come down from the summit straight to the car.

The short loop. At 6 turn left down the Badger Trail to the road. Just before the road, the northern half of the Rattlesnake Flats Trail leaves on the right and runs {{u:500 m|a third of a mile}} through the scrub to the Valencia Peak Trail; turn left there for the car park. 6.0 km in all, with about 175 m of climbing.

The peak alone. From the car park, cross the road and follow the Valencia Peak Trail up through 7 to 8 and back: 7.0 km, about 390 m of climbing, 2½–3 hours.

When to go. Any month. Spring brings the yellow flowers that gave Montaña de Oro, the Mountain of Gold, its name; grey whales pass from December to April. Spooner’s Cove is closed when the swell is high, as it was in late November 2026, so check the State Parks page that morning.

After the hike · 1

The otters of Morro Bay harbour

{{DETAIL}}

Morro Bay harbour. The letters are the three places to stand; the hike is {{u:18 km|11 miles}} to the south. Basemap: USGS The National Map.

Sea otters live in this harbour all year, and they keep close to the shore. Volunteers count them almost every month; the published counts of recent years run from about 40 to 70 adults. You watch from the {{u:pavement|sidewalk}}, at the same level as the animals and sometimes {{u:a few metres|a few yards}} from them.

DSouth T-Pier, just south of the Great American Fish Company. Otters feed along the bottom around the pier, and mothers with pups shelter beside it. Start here.
EThe Harborwalk, a level path along the water’s edge from the Embarcadero to Morro Rock, {{u:about a kilometre|a little over half a mile}} each way. Otters swim alongside it.
FTarget Rock, the big boulder at the entrance to the Morro Rock car park. Look down into the bay, away from the surf: rafts rest here.
{{PHOTO:o_pier}}
How to lookany hour; calm water is easiest

You are looking for a dark log about {{u:1.2 m|four feet}} long with a pale head at one end and two flippers sticking up at the other. If it is lying on its back, it is an otter: no other sea mammal rests belly-up as a habit. Watch a doubtful shape for half a minute, and an otter will roll, scrub its face or dive. Listen as well: the sharp clack, clack across the water is an otter breaking a shell on a stone held on its chest.

{{PHOTO:o_raft}}
Raftsresting, often near Target Rock F

Otters rest together in groups called rafts, forepaws on the chest and hind feet held clear of the water to keep them warm. An otter has no blubber. It has the densest fur of any animal, and it stays warm only while that fur holds a layer of air, so it spends hours a day grooming and eats about a quarter of its own weight daily.

{{PHOTO:o_pup}}
Mothers and pupsbeside the South T-Pier D

Pups are born in the water in every month. The town’s visitor bureau puts the local peaks from October to January and again in March and April. Look for a mother on her back with a ball of pale wool on her chest. A newborn’s fur holds so much air that it cannot dive; when she goes down to feed she leaves it bobbing on the surface. She nurses it for five to eight months.

Watching without disturbing. The shore is the best place to watch from, and the kindest. An otter that lifts its head to stare at you, stops eating or swims off has been disturbed, and every flight costs it warmth it must pay for in food. On the water, the US Fish and Wildlife Service asks paddlers to keep at least {{u:18 m|60 ft}}, five kayak lengths, from any otter, and asks everyone to keep dogs leashed around docks. Disturbing a sea otter is a federal offence.

Every sea otter in California descends from one group of a few dozen that survived the fur trade off Big Sur. The last full count, in 2019, found 2,962, and they are still listed as threatened.

After the hike · 2

Who lives here

{{PHOTO:sealion}}
California sea lionsharbour, all year · listen from E and F

The commonest mammal in visitors’ records of the harbour, and the loudest. They are brown, up to {{u:2.4 m|eight feet}} long, with small ear flaps, and they walk on their flippers. They rest on docks and boats, and the city built them a float of their own: look to the right of Morro Rock for three upright pilings. Harbour seals, smaller, spotted and earless, show a round head in the water and sink straight down.

{{PHOTO:falcon}}
Peregrine falconsMorro Rock, from F · nesting in spring

Peregrines nest on the cliffs of Morro Rock, which is a reserve for them and closed to climbing. Scan the upper faces for a crow-sized, dark-hooded bird on a ledge, and watch the gulls and pigeons: when they all go up at once, a falcon is hunting. Ospreys fish the harbour too, hovering and then dropping feet-first.

{{PHOTO:oyster}}
On the rocks and the mudbluffs 1 to 4 · harbour at low tide

Below the Bluff Trail the crow-sized black bird with a long orange-red bill is the black oystercatcher. The small black seabird with white wing patches and red feet is the pigeon guillemot, which nests in the cliffs in spring and summer. Cormorants dry their wings on the stacks and brown pelicans pass in lines. On the harbour’s sandbars at low tide, the tall brown waders with very long bills are long-billed curlews and marbled godwits; herons and egrets stand along the shore.

Whales, and the animals of the scrub. Grey whales pass the bluffs from December to April; look for a low, bushy blow. On land, mule deer, brush rabbits and California quail are the ones you are likely to meet, the quail running across the path in a line. Ground squirrels own the bluff edge. Bobcats and coyotes are recorded often, mostly early and late in the day.

A staircase of old shorelines

{{PHOTO:strata}}
Tilted bedsthe shore, 1 to 4

The rock under the Bluff Trail was laid down as flat layers of mud on the sea floor about six million years ago. Over the last three million years the layers have been tilted, and the sea now planes them off at an angle. The softer beds wear away first, which leaves the harder ones standing as the ribs and ledges that run out into the surf.

{{PHOTO:terraces}}
The stepsbest seen from 7 and 8

The level shelf you walked is an old sea floor: waves cut it about 80,000 years ago, and the land has since risen {{u:12 m|40 ft}}. The same thing happened before, again and again. Geologists count at least twelve such terraces on these slopes, each older, higher and more worn than the last, the highest a scrap {{u:250 m|820 ft}} up. From Valencia Peak they read as a flight of grassy steps down to the sea.

{{PHOTO:rock}}
Morro Rock{{u:176 m|576 ft}} · harbour mouth

The Rock is the hardened throat of a volcano about 21 million years old, the westernmost of a line of nine such plugs, the Nine Sisters, that runs inland to San Luis Obispo. The softer flanks have long since worn away. The bay behind it is far younger: a river valley drowned as the sea rose over the last 10,000 years, then closed off by the sand spit you saw from the peak. The causeway to the Rock is man-made.

Getting there, and on to the harbour

{{ACCESS}}

Solid blue: US 101 to the trailhead, 23 km (14 miles). Black dashes: trailhead to the harbour, 18 km (11 miles). Basemap: USGS The National Map. Routes: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

ALeave US 101 at Los Osos Valley Road, on the south side of San Luis Obispo, and follow it west for 17 km (11 mi).
BLos Osos. Note South Bay Boulevard on the right: it is your road to Morro Bay later. Keep straight on through the town (last food and fuel). The road becomes Pecho Valley Road and enters the park.
P6 km (4 mi) after the name changes, just past the Spooner Ranch House, the Bluff Trail car park is on the right. Any car is fine; the road is paved to its end.
CTo the harbour: back to B, left on South Bay Boulevard for 6 km (4 mi) across the head of the bay, then left on Quintana Road and left on Morro Bay Boulevard, which runs down through the town. Turn right on Main Street and left on Beach Street to the Embarcadero. 25 minutes.

Parking and fees. The park is free: no entrance or parking charge. It is open from 6 am to 10 pm. If the Bluff Trail car park is full, use the {{u:lay-bys|turnouts}} along the road or the Coon Creek car park at 5 and start the loop there.

Practical

  • Toilets at Spooner’s Cove and at 5, the Coon Creek car park. Carry your water. Maps and advice at the Spooner Ranch House: +1 805 539 6857.
  • Rules: no dogs on any trail. Stay on the path.
  • Poison oak (glossy leaves in threes, red in autumn) grows throughout the park. Check for ticks afterwards.
  • Park office: +1 805 772 6101. Emergency: 911.

Afterwards: dinner by the otters

This is the reason to finish at the harbour. The Embarcadero is a working waterfront of fishing boats and fish houses, and several of its tables look straight onto the water you have been watching. Kitchens close by 8:30 or 9 pm. Names are links to each restaurant’s own site.
