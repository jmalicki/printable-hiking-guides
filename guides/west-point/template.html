A half-day hike · Mount Tamalpais, Marin County, California

The balcony of Mount Tam

A loop along the sunny south face of the mountain, high above the fog: out on a contour path with San Francisco at your feet, lemonade on the porch of a 1904 railway inn, and home through cool forest.

Why this hike

This is the mountain’s greatest-hits loop, and the easiest way to understand why people who live here never leave. You start at {{u:600 m|nearly 2,000 ft}}, already above most of the Bay Area, so the views begin in the car park and do not stop for an hour.

  • The view. The outward leg is a balcony: the Pacific on your right, the city straight ahead, the bay and Mount Diablo to the left, often with a white sea of fog filling everything below you.
  • Five landscapes in two hours. Windy meadow, bare green rock, scented scrub, oak woodland, redwood ravines. They change every few hundred {{u:metres|yards}}, and page 4 explains why.
  • Two good stories. A stone amphitheatre built by hand in the 1930s, and the last surviving building of “the crookedest railroad in the world”.
  • A proper stop. The West Point Inn’s porch is the best-placed bench in Marin.

Is this for me?

Easier than the Muir Woods loop: shorter, with less climbing. If you can walk briskly for two hours you can do this.

Overall effortLike a brisk 10 km walk on the flat. About two hours of walking.
The shapeIt is an “upside-down” hike: you start at the top, lose height gently for 6 km, and pay it all back in the last 1.2 km.
The climb170 m up at an average 14%: 25–30 minutes of steady uphill, in shade. Then it is over.
UnderfootNarrow dirt paths, rocky in places on the first half; one short steep descent (Nora Trail). Shoes with grip.
SunThe first 3 km are fully exposed. Hat, sunscreen and water even on a cool day.

What you can see, and where

Rock Spring car parkWest over the grassy ridge to the Pacific. On a clear day the Farallon Islands sit on the horizon, 45 km out to sea.
Mountain Theater, top rowSouth-east over the stage: Richardson Bay, the Tiburon peninsula, Angel Island, the Bay Bridge and the East Bay hills, with Mount Diablo (1,173 m) behind.
Rock Spring TrailThe whole south face opens up. San Francisco’s towers above the Marin Headlands, with a tower of the Golden Gate Bridge showing over the ridge; below you the dark, forested canyon that holds Muir Woods; to the right the open ocean.
West Point Inn porchThe same panorama, sitting down. Look back up and left for the fire lookout on East Peak (784 m), the summit.
Matt Davis TrailMostly in forest, with windows: each time the path rounds an open spur you get the city and bay again, framed by trees.

In summer, fog often fills the bay to {{u:300–500 m|1,000–1,600 ft}}, and from this trail you look down on it: only bridge towers, the tallest buildings and hilltops stand clear.

The West Point Inn

From 1896 to 1930 a steam railway climbed Mount Tamalpais from Mill Valley, through 281 curves in 13 km: “the crookedest railroad in the world”. This was its westernmost point, where a stagecoach met the train to carry passengers down to Stinson Beach and Bolinas. The inn was built for them in 1904. The Old Stage Road you cross on the way home was the coach road; the Old Railroad Grade outside the door is the trackbed.

The inn survives, run by volunteers, with no mains electricity and no public road. The porch, picnic tables, toilets and drinking water are open to hikers by day, and simple drinks and snacks are often on sale: bring a little cash, but do not rely on it. You can stay the night, by reservation at westpointinn.com.

Field notes

Five worlds in {{u:seven kilometres|five miles}} — and why

On this loop the landscape changes like a slideshow: grass, bare rock, scrub, woodland, redwoods, sometimes within a hundred paces. None of it is random. Four things decide what grows on any square {{u:metre|yard}} of this mountain: how much summer sun it gets, whether the fog reaches it, what rock lies underneath, and when it last burned.

The setting: a Mediterranean climate beside a cold ocean

California’s coast has a Mediterranean climate: mild wet winters, and no rain at all from May to October. Every plant here has to solve the same problem: six months of drought. What sets this coast apart is the sea. The cold California Current chills the summer air into a bank of fog that pours through the Golden Gate most afternoons and banks up against the mountain to roughly {{u:300–500 m|1,000–1,600 ft}}. This trail runs along the top of that fog line, the border between a fog-watered world below and a sun-baked one above.

{{PHOTO:fog}}
{{PHOTO:grass}}
1 · Ridge-top grasslandRock Spring, {{u:km 0–0.5|the first 0.3 mi}}

Open meadow, gold from June, green from December, full of wildflowers in April. Wind, thin soil and summer drought keep trees out, but so did people: for thousands of years the Coast Miwok burned these ridges to keep them open for game and seed plants, and ranchers then grazed them. Without either, Douglas fir is slowly moving in.

{{PHOTO:serpentine}}
2 · Serpentine barrensaround the Theater, start of the Rock Spring Trail

The slick blue-green rock is serpentine, a piece of the Earth’s mantle scraped up at the plate boundary. It makes terrible soil: almost no calcium or nitrogen, and toxic levels of magnesium, nickel and chromium. Most plants cannot live on it, so it looks half-bare. Those that can are specialists found almost nowhere else: the stunted Sargent cypress, leather oak, and a manzanita that grows only on this mountain. Where the rock changes, the plants change along a line you could draw with a ruler.

{{PHOTO:chaparral}}
3 · ChaparralRock Spring Trail, the open south-facing slopes

Dense, head-high evergreen scrub: manzanita, chamise, ceanothus, scrub oak. It is California’s version of the scrub found around the Mediterranean, evolved separately for the same climate; the name comes from the Spanish chaparro, a scrub oak. South-facing slopes take the sun square-on all summer, so leaves here are small, thick and waxy, often held edge-on to the light, to save water. Chaparral is built to burn: every few decades fire clears it, and it regrows from buried root crowns and from seeds that germinate only after heat or smoke.

{{PHOTO:woodland}}
4 · Mixed evergreen woodlandevery ravine, and most of the Matt Davis Trail

Step round a corner onto a north- or east-facing fold and you are in shade: coast live oak, California bay laurel (crush a leaf: peppery, like strong bay), tanoak, Douglas fir, and madrone, with peeling orange bark. Arbutus menziesii is the big American cousin of the Mediterranean strawberry tree, the madroño, and Spanish missionaries named it for the resemblance. These folds get a fraction of the summer sun and hold moisture into August: aspect alone separates this from the scrub fifty {{u:metres|yards}} away.

{{PHOTO:redwood}}
5 · Redwood ravinescreek crossings on the Nora, Matt Davis and Bootjack trails

Coast redwoods, the tallest trees on Earth, need more water than the rain here provides, and they take it from the air. Their needles comb droplets out of the summer fog, which drip to the ground and can add a third or more to the year’s water. So redwoods grow only where fog reaches and soil is deep: on this loop, the stream gullies, near the upper limit of their range. The giants are 300 m below in Muir Woods; up here they are slimmer, each grove marking a spot where fog and water collect.

How special is this? Strictly, these are five habitats within one biome, the Mediterranean one, the rarest on Earth: its five regions cover about 2% of the world’s land but hold a sixth to a fifth of its plant species. California’s is one of 36 global biodiversity hotspots: about 3,500 plant species, 61% found nowhere else. Mount Tamalpais alone has some 750 native plants, six of which exist only here. Few hikes anywhere pack more botanical variety into a morning.

Who lives here. Turkey vultures and red-tailed hawks at eye level along the south face; fence lizards on the rocks; in the forest the crested, dark-blue Steller’s jay and, after rain, bright yellow banana slugs.

Getting to Rock Spring

{{ACCESS}}

Driving route from the Golden Gate Bridge: 26 km (16 miles). Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

Parking and gates

Rock Spring has a free car park with toilets; no reservation. It fills by mid-morning on fine weekends; there is more roadside parking along Ridgecrest Boulevard. The road gates are locked at sunset (the park is open 7:00 to sunset): be back at the car with time to spare.

Roadworks, November 2026: Pantoll Road is being resurfaced on weekdays until about 20 November, with one-way traffic control. Allow extra time on a weekday.

Rock Spring trailhead, junction of Pantoll Road and Ridgecrest Boulevard, Mount Tamalpais State Park. Allow 40 minutes from the Golden Gate Bridge, 55 from central San Francisco.

P2.3 km (1.4 mi) uphill, where Pantoll Road meets Ridgecrest Boulevard, the Rock Spring car park is directly ahead.

Without a car, or if Rock Spring is full

Marin Transit route 61 stops at Pantoll, ten minutes’ walk along the Matt Davis Trail from point 5. Start the loop there and walk it in the same direction, with the climb first and the inn two-thirds of the way round. Drivers can do the same from the Bootjack or Pantoll car parks (parking fee).

Practical

  • Water: taps at the Mountain Theater and the West Point Inn. Carry a litre each anyway.
  • Sun and wind: the first half has no shade and the ridge is often windy; the forest half can be {{u:8 °C|15 °F}} cooler. Bring a hat and a layer.
  • When: all year. April–May for wildflowers and green hills; summer for the sea of fog; autumn for the clearest air; winter after rain for running creeks (and mud on the Nora Trail).
  • Daylight: in winter the sun sets around 5 pm. Start by 1 pm at the latest.
  • Poison oak (glossy leaves in threes, red in autumn) lines the forest sections; do not touch. Check for ticks afterwards. Rattlesnakes sun themselves on the rocky first half in warm weather: watch your feet, and give one room.
  • No dogs or bikes on these trails. Leave nothing visible in the car. Emergency: 911; rangers +1 415 388 2070.

Afterwards

On the way down: the Mountain Home Inn is 5 km back along Panoramic Highway, with a deck over the bay. Casual food; check opening hours first (+1 415 381 9000).

In Mill Valley, 20 minutes down the hill (left at the four-way junction, E):

  • Playa — modern Mexican, patio, good margaritas. Closed Monday.
  • Sol Food — Puerto Rican, counter service, open all afternoon: the hungry hiker’s choice.
  • Bungalow 44 — lively Californian dinner and cocktails.
  • Buckeye Roadhouse — the classic 1937 roadhouse by the Highway 101 junction; book ahead.

Kitchens close around 9 pm. Hours change: check on the day.
