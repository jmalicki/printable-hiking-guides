A two-day backpacking loop · Grand Teton National Park, Wyoming

Behind the Grand Teton:
Paintbrush Divide and Lake Solitude

Everyone photographs these peaks from the road. Climb one canyon, cross a pass at {{u:3,260 m|10,700 ft}} and come down the next, and you see them from the side no road reaches, with moose in the willows on the way out. The pass keeps its snow into July, and you earn it.

{{PHOTO:cover}}

Why this hike

Most visitors take the boat across Jenny Lake, climb to Inspiration Point and turn round. Hikers go a few hours further up Cascade Canyon and come back the same way. This loop keeps going.

  • Two canyons and what is between them. Up Paintbrush through forest, flowers and boulder fields to a pass in bare rock; down to a lake under cliffs; out along a creek between walls {{u:a kilometre|over half a mile}} high.
  • The animals are the other half of it. Moose, black bears, pikas, marmots and the weasels that hunt them all live beside this trail (page 4).
  • Signed trail all the way, finishing where you parked.

Is this for me? Honestly

In August, on dry trail, this is a hard hike that any fit person can finish. Before the snow has gone from the pass it is a different trip, and not one for a first visit.

FitnessThe climb to the pass is 1,190 m in 12.7 km: about three Empire State Buildings, with a tent on your back. Running and cycling on the flat give you the engine for it, but not the legs for the 1,230 m of descent that follows.
AltitudeYou start at 2,100 m and sleep near 2,900 m. Coming from sea level, expect to be slower than the numbers suggest; a night or two in the valley first helps.
SkillOrdinary mountain hiking when dry: rocky, with steep, loose switchbacks on both sides of the pass. On snow the last climb needs an ice axe and the practice to stop a slide with it.
Easier optionHolly Lake and back (20.6 km, about 800 m of climb), or up Cascade Canyon to Lake Solitude and back: one canyon each, without the pass.

Before you go, and when to turn back

Turn back if hard snow covers the climb to Paintbrush Divide (between 3 and 4) and you do not have an ice axe and know how to use it. The Park Service says it often holds snow until mid-July and that an axe may be needed through July, in some years into August. Turn back, or wait below, if thunder is building: storms are common on summer afternoons and there is no shelter for an hour either side of the pass. Ask about conditions at a permit office the day before: +1 307 739 3309.

Grizzly and black bears live in both canyons. Carry bear spray where you can reach it (page 4). To camp you need a permit and a bear-proof food canister (page 5).

Why this way round. You meet the snow going up and early in the trip, when turning back costs half a day. Going the other way you would reach it late on a long climb and have to go down it.

If you cannot get a camp at Holly Lake

Permits decide where you sleep (page 5). Upper Paintbrush, the zone just above Holly Lake, gives the same two days. The North Fork of Cascade Canyon, below Lake Solitude, turns it round: about 18 km and all 1,190 m of climbing on day 1, with the pass in the afternoon, then an easy 12 km out. Take it only with a dawn start and a settled forecast.

The loop in one day

No permit needed, and many strong hikers do it: the Park Service allows 11–14 hours. Start at first light with a head torch, be over the pass by noon, and carry spray, a filter and a warm layer. It is too long for a first day at this altitude.

Why the canyons look like this

A fault made the wall. The range is a single block of the Earth’s crust, tilted up along the Teton fault at its eastern foot while the valley floor dropped. It has been rising for less than 10 million years, and the total offset is probably more than {{u:7,600 m|25,000 ft}}. That is why the mountains stand straight up from the flats where you parked.

The rock is far older than the mountains. The banded grey rock of the canyon walls is gneiss, 2.7 billion years old, shot through with paler granite of about 2.5 billion. The black stripes up some faces are dikes of diabase, about 775 million years old.

Ice cut the canyons. A river cuts a V. Paintbrush and Cascade Canyons are U-shaped, flat-floored and steep-sided, because glaciers filled them in the last ice age, which the Park Service dates to about 50,000 to 12,000 years ago. Holly Lake and Lake Solitude lie in basins the ice scooped at the canyon heads.

The lakes at the bottom are its leftovers. The glacier from Cascade Canyon spilled onto the valley floor and dropped a ring of rubble at its snout. When the ice melted, the hollow inside the ring filled with water: Jenny Lake.

Field notes

Who lives here, and where on the loop to look

The loop climbs from lakeshore to bare rock and back, and each band has its own animals. Go quietly early and late in the day, look into the boulder fields as well as at the peaks, and keep your distance: at least {{u:90 m (100 yards)|100 yards}} from bears and wolves and {{u:23 m (25 yards)|25 yards}} from everything else. That is the park’s rule. If an animal moves off because of you, you were too close. Feeding any of them, ground squirrels included, is illegal.

{{PHOTO:grizzly}}
Bears: two kindsanywhere on the loop, at any hour

Black bears have always used these canyons and grizzlies now occupy the whole park. Colour tells you nothing: both run from blond to black. A grizzly has a hump over the shoulders, a dished face, short round ears and long, nearly straight front claws. A black bear has no hump, a straight line from nose to forehead and taller ears. Grizzlies are much the heavier: males {{u:180–360 kg|400–790 lb}} against about {{u:120 kg|260 lb}}. The Yellowstone region held about 1,000 grizzlies in 2023, against some 300 in the late 1960s.

{{PHOTO:blackbear}}
If you meet onethe park’s advice, in short

Hike in a group and make noise where you cannot see ahead. Do not run. Back away slowly, talking. Keep the spray on your belt or chest strap, not in the pack, and use it at about {{u:9 m|30 ft}} if the bear comes on. If a bear makes contact, lie face down with your hands over your neck and your pack on. If one stalks you, or attacks at night, fight.

{{PHOTO:moose}}
Moosewillows beside Cascade Creek, 6 to 7; mouth of the canyon

The largest deer on Earth: a bull stands {{u:1.7–2.3 m|5½–7½ ft}} at the shoulder and weighs close to {{u:450 kg|1,000 lb}}, with antlers at full size by August. They eat willow, up to {{u:23 kg|50 lb}} of it a day in summer, and they dislike heat, so look in the shade and the wet. They look placid and are not: a cow with a calf will charge. If one is on the trail, wait or go round at a distance. The local herd is small and falling: 289 were counted in 2026. Mule deer browse the canyon mouth too.

{{PHOTO:pika}}
Pikaboulder fields in both canyons; listen first

A short, sharp “eep” from a pile of rocks is a pika: a round-eared relative of the rabbit that fits in your hand, {{u:18–21 cm|7–8 in}} long, with no visible tail. It does not hibernate. All summer it cuts plants and dries them in haystacks, up to {{u:a metre|3 ft}} across, to eat under the snow. It suffers above about {{u:25 °C|78 °F}}, so on a hot day it stays below the rocks until evening.

{{PHOTO:marmot}}
Yellow-bellied marmotthe same boulders, sunbathing on top of them

The big one: a ground squirrel the size of a cat, up to {{u:5 kg|11 lb}}, with a loud single whistle when it sees you, a hawk or a coyote. It sleeps for up to eight months of the year, so everything you see it do in August is eating. The small striped beggars at Inspiration Point are golden-mantled ground squirrels (plain face) and chipmunks (striped face).

{{PHOTO:weasel}}
Weaselsboulder fields and meadows; a quick brown streak

Whatever lives in the rocks, a weasel hunts it. The park has two common kinds, both brown in summer, white in winter, with a black tail tip all year. The short-tailed weasel (the stoat of Britain, the ermine of the fur trade) is {{u:20–33 cm|8–13 in}} long and white underneath. The long-tailed weasel is {{u:33–46 cm|13–18 in}}, with a buff or orange belly and a tail half its length. Both are slim enough to follow a vole down its burrow, and both take pikas.

{{PHOTO:marten}}
Martenconifer forest of the lower canyons, 2 and 7

The weasel of the forest: cat-sized, {{u:45–65 cm|18–26 in}} long, brown with a buff or orange patch on the throat and a bushy tail. It hunts voles and squirrels among fallen logs, mostly on the ground, and takes pikas where the trees meet the rocks. A marten burns so much energy that it needs about three voles a day.

{{PHOTO:otter}}
Mink and river otterlake shores and outlets, 1 and 8; early and late

The two swimmers of the family. The mink is dark, weasel-shaped and never far from the water’s edge; it can swim {{u:30 m|100 ft}} under water. The otter is several times larger, {{u:over a metre|40–54 in}} long with a thick tapering tail, and stays under for two or three minutes. Two more relatives you are unlikely to meet on the trail: the badger lives in the sagebrush you drive through, and the wolverine is rare anywhere in the range.

Permits and where you may camp

A day hike needs no permit. Every night out needs a backcountry permit for a named zone, and these two canyons are the most requested in the park.

  • In advance: about a third of the places, on recreation.gov. Booking for May to October 2027 opens on 11 January 2027 at 8 am Mountain Time, and the popular zones go quickly. $20 per permit plus $7 per person per night.
  • In person: the other two thirds, first come, first served, no earlier than the day before you start. Be at the door when it opens.
  • Where: the Jenny Lake Ranger Station (June to early September), the Craig Thomas visitor centre at Moose, or Colter Bay. A reserved permit must also be collected in person, by 10 am on the day you start.
  • You give your car’s plate number: overnight parking at the trailhead is allowed only with a permit.

Rules: up to 6 people on a permit. Food, rubbish and toiletries go in an approved bear canister, lent free with the permit; hanging food is not allowed. Cook and store it {{u:90 m|100 yards}} downwind of the tent. No fires in the canyons: stoves only. No dogs on any trail.

Water

Where it is

  • The trailhead. The park’s own pages disagree on whether there is a tap at String Lake. Arrive with full bottles.
  • Day 1. The lakes for the first hour, then Paintbrush Creek and its side streams beside the trail through the lower canyon, and Holly Lake and its outlet.
  • Over the pass. Streams and two small tarns in the upper canyon. From the foot of the headwall to Lake Solitude, about 5 km and two to three hours, I found no report of water once the snow has gone. Carry 1.5 litres each from the last stream.
  • Day 2, after the pass. Lake Solitude, then a creek beside you all the way to Jenny Lake.
  • The Park Service calls water in these canyons plentiful and does not list them among the places that dry up in late summer. I found no dated report of a source failing.

How clean, and what to do to it

  • Clear. Snowmelt off hard rock. I found no report of silt, colour or glacial flour, so nothing should need settling first; the Park Service says nothing either way.
  • Treat all of it. The Park Service warns of Giardia and Campylobacter and says to boil, filter or use tablets.
  • Upstream. These are very busy trails in July and August, with horse parties on the main canyon trails (the Holly Lake branch is closed to them). Draw from moving water above the crossings and camps.
  • Algae. I found no toxic-algae warning for any lake in the park in 2026.

When to go, and what to bring

  • Season: late July to mid-September for a dry pass in a normal year. Valley trails clear by mid-June, the canyons and passes not until late July. Reports found the Divide free of snow on 20 July 2022 and on 11 September 2023, and impassable without axe and crampons on 21 June 2025. Snow can return in September.
  • Weather: thunderstorms build on summer afternoons. Stay off the pass, ridges and open ground when they do. Expect nights near freezing at Holly Lake.
  • Flowers: the canyon is named for the red Indian paintbrush, Wyoming’s state flower. The high meadows are usually at their best from mid-July into August.
  • Bear spray, one can each, bought or rented in Jackson or at Jenny Lake. It cannot go on a plane.
  • Paper map and the GPX on your phone; do not count on a phone signal in the canyons.
  • Filter or tablets; warm layer, hat, gloves and waterproof; sun hat and sunscreen (thin air burns fast); poles for the descent.
  • Before mid-July, or after a snowy winter: an ice axe, and crampons or spikes, per the rangers’ advice that week.
  • Insect repellent in early summer. Step off on the uphill side for horses.

Getting to String Lake

{{ACCESS}}

Driving route from Jackson: 40 km (25 miles), about 45 minutes. Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

From Jackson, the town at the south end of the valley, with the shops, hotels and gear rental. Jackson Hole Airport is inside the park, between Jackson and Moose. Paved all the way: any car should be fine.

JJackson. Take US 26/89/191 north past the elk refuge and the airport turning.
MAfter 20 km (12 mi), at Moose Junction, turn left onto Teton Park Road. You pass the Craig Thomas visitor centre and the entrance station, where you pay.
SSouth Jenny Lake, 13 km on: visitor centre, ranger station (permits), shop. Keep straight on.
N17 km (11 mi) from Moose Junction, at North Jenny Lake Junction, turn left onto the Jenny Lake scenic loop.
1After 2.4 km (1.5 mi) turn right onto String Lake Road. The first car park is the String Lake trailhead; the canoe launch and the Leigh Lake trailhead and picnic area are further along, and all three serve this loop. Toilets at the car parks.

Parking, fees and the season

  • Parking is free but short: 226 spaces between the three car parks, usually full from 11 am to 3 pm in summer, and no parking on the roadside. Arrive before 8:00.
  • Entrance: $35 per car for seven days (2026). Visitors who are not US residents pay a further $100 per person aged 16 or over at this park, unless they hold an annual pass ($250 for non-residents, $80 for residents). Check nps.gov/grte/planyourvisit/fees.htm before you travel.
  • Teton Park Road is closed to cars from 1 November to 30 April between Taggart Lake and Signal Mountain. The loop is a summer trip.

Leave no food or scented things in the car.

Without a car

Plan on a car or a taxi from Jackson. The Jenny Lake boat lands at South Jenny Lake, 4.5 km on foot from String Lake.

Afterwards

Dornan’s at Moose, on your way back to Jackson, has a pizza and pasta restaurant with the range across the river. Signal Mountain Lodge, 20 minutes north, has a grill and bar on Jackson Lake from May to early October. Jenny Lake Lodge, beside the trailhead, serves dinner by reservation only. In Jackson, the Snake River Grill (84 East Broadway) is open nightly from 5 to 9 pm: kitchens here close early, so do not linger at the lake.
