A full-day hike · Yellowstone National Park, Montana

To the edge of Yellowstone:
Bighorn Peak by Black Butte Creek

Follow a creek out of the sagebrush, climb through forest and flower meadows, and come out on the grassy rim of the park, where the ground stops at a line of cliffs. Elk bugle below you, on a mountain built of buried forests. You pay with {{u:a thousand metres|3,300 feet}} of climbing in grizzly country.

{{PHOTO:hero}}

Why this hike

Most of Yellowstone is seen from a road or a boardwalk, in company. Its best-known summit, Mount Washburn, is climbed on an old service road. This corner of the park has no geysers, no entrance gate and no boardwalk. It has a mountain, and one trail that goes from the highway to the top.

  • A whole mountain in a day. You start among willows and sagebrush at 2,080 m and finish on bare rock at {{u:3,027 m|9,930 ft}}, passing through every kind of country in between.
  • A summit on the rim. The top is on the park boundary. Behind you is Yellowstone; in front the ground drops away and three mountain ranges fill the horizon.
  • A forest turned to stone. The mountain is made of the debris that buried forest after forest, fifty million years ago. Their trunks still stand in the cliffs (page 4).
  • Wild company. Elk bugling in September, grouse on the trail, bear tracks in the mud (page 5).

Is this for me? Honestly

One long climb with no flat stretch to rest on and no water in its upper half. Anyone fit can do it at a steady pace. You also need to be at ease hiking where grizzly bears live.

FitnessIf you can run or cycle for two hours you have the engine. The climb equals two and a half Empire State Buildings, and it gets steeper as it goes: the last 1.5 km gains 250 m. Flat-ground fitness does not train the way down, which is the same {{u:1,000 m|3,300 ft}} on tired legs. The top is above 3,000 m: coming from sea level you will be slower and shorter of breath than you expect.
The topThe trail reaches a broad meadow at {{u:3,013 m|9,888 ft}}, which already has the view. The true summit is {{u:360 m|400 yards}} further along a rocky crest with cliffs beside it. No hands are needed, but it is not a place for anyone unhappy near a drop.
Shorter optionTurn round at the footbridge (3): 9.8 km there and back with 340 m of climbing, 3 hours, through the meadows where elk and moose are likeliest.

Read this first

Bear spray, and company. The Park Service says grizzly bears frequent this area and asks hikers to go in groups of three or more. Each adult carries a can on the belt and knows how to use it (page 5). Make noise where you cannot see ahead, and hike in daylight. If that worries you more than it interests you, choose a busier trail.

Be off the top by early afternoon. The last 1.5 km is open meadow and bare crest, and summer storms bring lightning. A hiker on this rim in August 2020 was caught by hail and lightning in mid-afternoon on a day forecast at 15% chance of storms. Turn back if you hear thunder, if clouds are building tall by late morning, or if snow or ice still lies on the crest.

Step by step

{{u:km|miles}} · heightWhat to do, and what to look for
10.0 km2,080 m Black Butte trailhead. Park in the gravel pull-out on the west side of the highway and cross the road with care: traffic passes at 55 mph. The trail starts on the east side at a small brown sign reading “Black Butte”, with a notice board just inside the trees. It climbs from the first step, with the creek below on your right.
0–3 km+180 m Small meadows, then old forest, then meadows again, always near Black Butte Creek. Black Butte itself is the steep-sided hill across the creek to the south-east. Early and late in the day these meadows are the place for elk, mule deer and moose (page 5). Call out before blind corners and thick willows.
23.0 km2,260 m Daly Creek cutoff junction, in a large meadow full of tall white flowers in July. A signpost gives distances in miles and kilometres. The cutoff trail goes left to Daly Creek. Keep straight on for Bighorn Peak. Two minutes later a spur drops right to campsite WF1; keep left.
34.9 km2,420 m The footbridge over Black Butte Creek. This is the last water: from here to the top and back is 10 km and nearly 600 m of climbing with none, most of it in the sun. Fill up (page 4). Beyond the bridge the trail turns away from the creek and steepens.
5–8 km2,430–2,760 m The switchbacks, up a spur of the mountain through forest and open slopes. As you gain height, look north across the valley to the grey cliffs of King Butte: the brown upright shapes among them are petrified tree trunks (page 4). The trail then crosses an old burn, with standing dead trees and thick flowers in July. Guidebooks call this stretch the likeliest for bighorn sheep. Behind you the Madison Range rises into view.
8–9.6 km2,760–3,010 m The trail gains a ridge with scattered trees, then climbs a steep open meadow, 250 m in the last 1.5 km. Near the top the worn path fades into the grass. Follow the posts and cairns straight up the slope. Look back and fix the line in your mind for the descent.
49.6 km{{u:3,013 m|9,888 ft}} Sky Rim junction, on a broad grass summit with a signpost and park boundary markers. The Sky Rim Trail runs both ways along the crest. This is a fine place to stop: the views are nearly those of the summit. The sign gives 7.0 miles back to the trailhead; the mapped trail measures {{u:9.6 km|6.0 miles}}. For the true top turn right, east.
510.0 km{{u:3,027 m|9,930 ft}} Bighorn Peak. The trail follows a rocky crest for {{u:360 m|400 yards}} to the highest point. There are steep drops beside it and some loose stones underfoot; take it slowly and stay on the path. Look down the cliffs for sheep and for white mountain goats.
119.9 km2,080 m Going back, the same way. In the meadow below 4 the path is hard to see from above: follow the posts, and keep the cliffs on your left as you start down. Refill at the bridge. Make noise in the last hour through the meadows, when you are tired, quiet and it is getting late.

A mountain made of buried forests

Fifty million years ago this was warm, wet country with volcanoes to the east. The forests were closer to a subtropical rainforest than to anything here now: sequoia, pine and cypress, with sycamore, magnolia, walnut and relatives of oak.

Then the volcanoes buried them. Flows of mud, ash and boulders came down the valleys and set around the trunks. Water carrying dissolved silica soaked the buried wood and replaced it, cell by cell, with stone. A new forest grew on top and was buried in turn. Geologists of the US Geological Survey counted more than 30 such levels in the rock unit that forms this mountain, with many trunks over {{u:1.2 m|4 ft}} across still standing upright.

What you are looking at. Bighorn Peak is where that rock unit was first described. On its slopes you can pick out two dark-grey bands of cliff, made of coarse volcanic rubble, with a pale, crumbling band between them. Each is a pile of those flows.

Grown here or washed in? For a century the stacked stumps were read as forests buried where they stood. In 1980 the geologist William Fritz argued that some had been carried upright by the flows, as happened at Mount St. Helens. His own later estimate was that about 85% of Yellowstone’s upright stumps are where they grew.

{{PHOTO:petrified}}{{PHOTO:flank}}

Where to see the wood. From the switchbacks, on the cliffs of King Butte to the north: brown columns among grey rock. Binoculars help. Along the Sky Rim there are trunks beside the trail itself, and hikers report pale fragments lying on the slopes below them.

Why this corner is in the park, and what that means for your pockets. In 1929 the park boundary was moved to take in the north-west corner, in the Park Service’s words “a significant area of petrified trees”. Inside the park it is illegal to remove or disturb any fossil, rock or plant, however small the piece. Leave everything where it lies for the next person to find.

When to go

  • July to September. Snow lingers on the upper mountain into July, and the park advises against the exposed ridge while snow or ice remains. I found no dated early-season report for this trail, so before mid-July phone the backcountry office: +1 307 344 2160.
  • July and early August for flowers, with the most afternoon storms. September for settled weather, elk calling in the meadows and cold mornings. A party that reached the top on 24 October 2015 found fresh snow and a summit near freezing.
  • Start by 7:00, to be on top by late morning and down off the open ground before storms build.
  • In early September bighorn sheep are hunted in the national forest on the north side of the rim. Hunting is not allowed in the park, but you may meet hunters on the crest.

Water

  • Carry {{u:3 litres|3 quarts (3 liters)}} each from the car. There is no tap at the trailhead.
  • The creek is beside or near the trail for the first 4.9 km. Above the footbridge there is none: the sign on top says “no water” for the Sky Rim in both directions. Treat creek water with a filter or tablets.

Bring

  • Bear spray, one can per adult, worn where you can reach it. It is sold in the towns around the park and cannot go on a plane.
  • Sun hat, sunscreen, and a warm layer and windproof for the top.
  • Food for a full day. Shoes with grip; poles help on the way down.
  • Binoculars, for sheep, goats and stone trees.
  • A paper map or the GPX on your phone, and a headtorch. Do not count on a phone signal.

Rules and fees

  • No permit for a day hike. Camping at site WF1 needs a backcountry permit: $5 per person per night plus a $10 booking fee, from recreation.gov or a park backcountry office.
  • Park entry is $35 per car for seven days. Since 2026 visitors who are not US residents pay a further $100 per person aged 16 or over, unless they hold an annual pass. There is no entrance station on this highway, and through traffic pays nothing. I could not confirm how the park expects hikers who stop here to pay: if you are visiting the rest of Yellowstone, keep your pass in the car; if this is your only stop, ask the park on +1 307 344 7381.
  • Stay on the trail. No dogs, bicycles or drones. No collecting.
  • Wildlife: keep {{u:90 m (100 yards)|100 yards}} from bears and wolves and {{u:23 m (25 yards)|25 yards}} from everything else.

Who lives on this mountain

On this trail you hear animals and find their traces more often than you watch them. Three things set it apart: a lot of bears, elk that bugle in autumn, and grouse. They come first below, in the order you are likely to notice them. If you meet a bear: do not run. Stay together, talk to it calmly and back away. If it charges, use the spray when it is {{u:9–18 m|30–60 ft}} off. If a grizzly makes contact, lie face down with your hands behind your neck and your pack on.

{{PHOTO:grizzly}}
Bears: two kindsanywhere on the trail, at any hour

“Grizzly bears frequent this area” is the Park Service’s own line for this trailhead, and bears are the most often recorded large animal in this corner of the park. About 1,050 grizzlies live in the Yellowstone region, against 136 in 1975. Black bears are here too. Colour tells you nothing. A grizzly has a hump over the shoulders, a rump lower than the hump, a dished face and long straight claws; males weigh {{u:90–320 kg|200–700 lb}}. A black bear has no hump, a rump higher than its shoulders and a straight nose.

{{PHOTO:track}}
Reading the groundmud by the creek, 1 to 3; the trail itself

You are more likely to find where a bear has been than the bear. The park asks you to watch for tracks, droppings, claw marks on trees and patches of dug-up ground, and to keep well away from any dead animal. In a grizzly track the toes sit close together in a nearly straight line, with claw marks {{u:5–10 cm|2–4 in}} ahead of them. A black bear’s toes are spread in an arc and its short claws often leave no mark. A party on the rim in August 2025 found a fresh grizzly track on the trail. Fresh sign means talk louder and stay together.

{{PHOTO:elk}}
Elkcreek meadows, 1 to 3; heard all the way up the valley

From early September to mid-October the bulls bugle: a deep note that climbs fast to a high, thin squeal and ends in a run of grunts. It advertises to the cows and challenges other bulls, and it carries a long way in still morning air. A bull weighs about {{u:320 kg|700 lb}} and stands {{u:1.5 m|5 ft}} at the shoulder; his antlers, grown new each summer, weigh {{u:14 kg|30 lb}} the pair. Keep {{u:23 m|25 yards}} away, and more in the rut, when bulls charge people.

{{PHOTO:dusky}}
Dusky grouseforest edges and open slopes, from 2 to the ridge

A grey-brown bird the size of a small chicken, {{u:47–57 cm|18–22 in}} long, with a long, square, dark tail: the third-largest grouse in North America. In summer hens lead their chicks through grass and flowers well away from the trees. A hen with chicks may flutter off as if injured, or fly straight at you, to draw you away. Males are slate-grey with a yellow-orange comb over the eye. Dusky grouse winter high on the mountain, in the conifers, living on the needles.

{{PHOTO:ruffed}}
Ruffed grousebrush and trees along the creek, 1 to 3

Smaller, {{u:40–48 cm|16–19 in}}, with a small crest, a black ruff on the neck and a broad black band near the tip of the fanned tail. It keeps to dense, brushy cover near stream bottoms. In spring the male stands on a log and beats his wings on the air, as in the photo. The sound starts slowly and speeds up like an engine trying to start, and carries {{u:400 m|a quarter of a mile}}.

{{PHOTO:moose}}
Moose and mule deerwillows at the car park and along the lower creek; dawn and dusk

Fewer than 200 moose live in the park, and the Gallatin River valley is one of the places the Park Service names for seeing them; it is the most often recorded mammal around this trailhead. Look in the willows by the river before you start. A bull stands {{u:1.7–2.3 m|5½–7½ ft}} at the shoulder. Give any moose a wide berth: a cow with a calf will charge. Mule deer have very large ears and a black-tipped tail on a white rump.

{{PHOTO:sheep}}
Bighorn sheep and mountain goatscliffs and ledges below 4 and 5; bring binoculars and patience

The peak is named for the sheep, and a herd that winters near Gardiner summers in the high country around it: 89 were counted in 2014. Do not count on meeting them. None of the dated trip reports behind this guide saw one. Rams carry thick curled horns of up to {{u:18 kg|40 lb}}; ewes have short, thin ones. Anything all white on the cliffs is a mountain goat, descended from animals released in Montana in the 1940s and 1950s.

{{PHOTO:marmot}}
The smaller onesthinly spread here; a bonus when you see one

Yellow-bellied marmots, cat-sized ground squirrels that whistle from a boulder, are the likeliest. Chipmunks and Uinta ground squirrels work the lower meadows, a badger has been recorded digging there, and river otters and coyotes along the Gallatin. A mountain lion has been recorded too. The range also holds the rest of the weasel family (short-tailed and long-tailed weasels, marten, and wolverine along this boundary) and pikas in broken rock, but few hikers here meet them.

Getting to the trailhead

{{ACCESS}}

Solid blue: from West Yellowstone, 46 km (29 miles). Dashes: from the Big Sky turn, 31 km (19 miles). Basemap: USGS The National Map. Routes: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

Black Butte trailhead is on US Highway 191, the main road between West Yellowstone and Bozeman, where it cuts through the north-west corner of the park. The road is paved and open all year: any car should be fine. There is no entrance gate on it.

WFrom West Yellowstone, take US 191 north towards Bozeman for 46 km (29 mi), about 35 minutes. The first part is national forest; the park begins further on. The Specimen Creek trailhead, on the right, comes 3.7 km (2.3 mi) before yours.
BFrom Big Sky, at the junction of Highway 64 and US 191, drive south on 191 for 31 km (19 mi), about 25 minutes. The park boundary is 4 km (2.5 mi) before the trailhead, and you pass the Daly Creek trailhead on the way. From Bozeman it is 97 km (60 mi) and about 1¼ hours.
PThe pull-out is on the west side of the road, the side nearer the river, and is not signed. The trail starts across the highway, about {{u:100 m|100 yards}} to the north.

On the road. The limit inside the park is 55 mph and is enforced. Animals cross here: in one evening in May 2023 two bears were killed by vehicles on this stretch. Drive slowly at dawn and dusk. There is no fuel between Big Sky and West Yellowstone.

Parking

Free, on gravel, with a notice board at the trail and nothing else: no toilet and no tap. Leave no food visible in the car.

Without a car

Not practical. Skyline buses run between Bozeman and Big Sky, and nothing serves the highway south of there.

Afterwards

  • Going north: the Corral Bar and Steakhouse, on the highway in the Gallatin Canyon south of the Big Sky turn; kitchen until 9 pm, closed early in the week. Riverhouse BBQ, beside the river at Big Sky, serves supper from 3 pm every night of the year and takes no bookings.
  • Going south: in West Yellowstone, Wild West Pizzeria is open 11 am to 10 pm daily.
