A day hike · Purisima Creek Redwoods Preserve, on the Skyline ridge above Half Moon Bay

The redwoods that came back

Drop from the ridge into a canyon that was cut to the ground a century ago and has grown itself a forest again, follow its creek, cross a hidden ravine on a footpath, and climb back out with the Pacific behind you. It is an upside-down hike: everything you walk down in the morning, you walk up in the afternoon.

Why this hike

The famous redwood parks are old growth, boardwalks and tour buses. This is the other kind of redwood forest, the kind most of the coast actually has: logged out between 1854 and 1920, left alone, and now a deep, quiet, hundred-year-old wood growing round the stumps of the trees that built San Francisco.

  • Down a gulch of ferns and firs. Whittemore Gulch drops from open ridge-top scrub, with the ocean in view, into deep forest.
  • A creek to climb beside. An old logging road rises gently along the water for an hour, under redwoods, maples and ferns.
  • A footpath to yourself. The Craig Britton Trail, closed to bikes and horses, contours through the best of the forest before the last pull up the ridge.

Is this for me?

A full day, and the hard half is the second half. A sign at the trailhead warns that people who walked down have needed rescuing. Be honest about the climb back.

Overall effortLike a brisk 22 km walk on the flat: about five hours of walking.
The way down480 m of descent in the first 5 km, most of it on the switchbacks of Whittemore Gulch. Easy on the lungs, hard on the knees.
The way up670 m of climbing in the last 9.7 km. The creek road is gentle and shaded; the final 2 km gain 190 m on an open ridge, in the sun, when you are tired.
ShorterFrom the canyon floor, straight back up Harkins Ridge: 10.4 km and 550 m of climbing (page 3).

No dogs are allowed in this preserve. There is no drinking water, and phone signal is patchy at best. The preserve is open from half an hour before sunrise to half an hour after sunset, free.

Why this way round

Whittemore Gulch is the better trail to descend: switchbacks, and the sea in front of you at the top. Coming back by the creek and the Craig Britton Trail spreads the climb over shaded, even ground and leaves only the last 2 km steep.

When to go

All year. The canyon is cool and often foggy in summer while the ridge can be hot; winter brings mud, a loud creek and newts on the trail, and Whittemore Gulch is then closed to bikes and horses, which suits walkers. After big storms check Midpen’s trail alerts.

Junctions are signed with trail names. Five trails make the loop: North Ridge, Whittemore Gulch, Purisima Creek, Craig Britton, Harkins Ridge.

Shorter: straight up the ridge · 10.4 km, 550 m of climbing

At 4, instead of going up the creek, take the Harkins Ridge Trail (blue dashes): a wide, steep road that climbs 330 m in 3.4 km to 8, mostly in the open. You miss the creek and the Craig Britton Trail and save about 1½ hours. A short walk up the level first part of the creek trail and back is worth adding.

Starting at the bottom instead

The lower trailhead at 4 lets you climb first and finish downhill, and is reached from Half Moon Bay by Higgins Canyon Road. But it has seven parking spaces and no overflow, so it works only on weekdays or very early.

Field notes

A forest grown from stumps

The sawmills. In 1854, five years after the Gold Rush began, the first sawmill went up at the mouth of this canyon. San Francisco was being built of wood, and redwood splits cleanly into shingles. Over the next seventy years at least seven mills worked along the creek. Loggers stood on planks wedged into notches in the trunk to cut above the swollen base; oxen, and later steam winches, dragged the logs down greased skid roads to the mills. One mill owner, Purdy Pharis, was known as the Shingle King. The pay was a dollar a day and board.

By the early 1900s the trees that were easy to reach were gone, and the last mill closed about 1920. What you walk up is the loggers’ road.

Sprouts from the roots. A redwood does not die when it is felled. It sprouts from the roots, which is why you see young trees in rings round the old stumps. A century on, those sprouts are tall trees: slender, close together, all of an age. The originals may have been a thousand years old. Redwoods get much of their summer water from fog, which condenses on the needles and drips to the ground; that is why they keep to the wet side of these hills and to the canyons.

The preserve. Selective logging went on in parts of these hills into the 1970s. The preserve began in the 1980s with a $2 million gift from the Save the Redwoods League to the open-space district, and has grown since to 5,412 acres (2,190 hectares).

Who lives here now. Banana slugs, bright yellow and as long as your hand, cross the trails in damp weather. From November to March newts walk to their breeding pools: do not pick them up, their skin is poisonous. The creek has trout but no salmon or steelhead, because a waterfall where it meets the sea has always kept them out. And in 2022 biologists again recorded marbled murrelets here in numbers: a threatened seabird that feeds on the ocean and nests on the mossy upper branches of big old conifers. Jays and ravens drawn by food scraps raid those nests, so carry every crumb out.

The number of mills is given as six, seven or eight in different accounts, and Pharis’s output as 70,000 or 100,000 shingles a day. The fog figure comes from a study of redwoods further north, not from this canyon.

The name

The creek has been called after la Purísima Concepción, the Immaculate Conception, since Spanish times. A town called Purissima stood near its mouth from the 1850s; it was deserted by the 1930s, and little but its cemetery is left.

Practical

  • Water and toilets: toilets at the car park and at the lower trailhead, halfway; no water anywhere. Bring 2 litres each.
  • Shared trails: the creek and ridge roads are open to bikes and horses. Bikes come down Whittemore Gulch (outside winter) and Harkins Ridge fast; keep right. The Craig Britton Trail is for walkers only.
  • Layers: the canyon floor is cold and damp even when Half Moon Bay is sunny, and the ridge can be hot an hour later.
  • Watch for poison oak (glossy leaves in threes) beside the sunny upper trails, and ticks; check yourself afterwards. Rattlesnakes sun on the ridge in warm weather. Mountain lions live here and are very rarely seen.
  • Emergency: 911. Midpen office: +1 650 691 1200.
