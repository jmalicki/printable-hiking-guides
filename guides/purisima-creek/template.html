A half-day hike · Purisima Creek Redwoods Preserve, above Half Moon Bay

The redwoods that came back

Follow a creek up a canyon that was cut to the ground a century ago and has grown itself a forest again, cross a hidden ravine on a footpath, and come down a ridge with the Pacific in front of you. The catch is at the start: the car park holds seven cars.

Why this hike

The famous redwood parks are old growth, boardwalks and tour buses. This is the other kind of redwood forest, the kind most of the coast actually has: logged out between 1854 and 1920, left alone, and now a deep, quiet, hundred-year-old wood growing round the stumps of the trees that built San Francisco.

  • A creek the whole way up. An old logging road climbs gently beside the water for an hour, under redwoods, maples and ferns.
  • A footpath to yourself. The Craig Britton Trail, closed to bikes and horses, contours through the best of the forest and out into the sun.
  • The ocean on the way down. Harkins Ridge drops towards Half Moon Bay with the coast ahead of you, fog permitting.

Is this for me?

A proper half-day with one long, gentle climb and one steep descent. If you can walk briskly for three hours, yes.

Overall effortLike a brisk 16 km walk on the flat: about 3½ hours of walking.
The climbAbout 480 m in all, spread over the first 7.7 km. The creek road is gentle for the first 1.6 km, then has stretches of 10–15%. Nothing is steep for long, and almost all of it is in shade.
The descent350 m in 3.4 km down Harkins Ridge, on a wide dirt road that is loose in places. Hard on knees; shoes with grip help.
ParkingSeven spaces and a little roadside at the trailhead, and no other car park nearby. Come on a weekday or early. Plan B, from the top, is on page 3.

No dogs are allowed in this preserve. There is no drinking water, and phone signal is patchy at best. The preserve is open from half an hour before sunrise to half an hour after sunset, free.

Why this way round

Going up the creek first puts the climb in the shade on an even grade, and saves the views for the descent, when they are in front of you. The other way round starts with the steep ridge in the sun.

When to go

All year. The canyon is cool and often foggy in summer while the ridge can be hot; winter brings mud, a loud creek and newts on the trail. After big storms check Midpen’s trail alerts: in 2023 landslides closed this trailhead for months.

Step by step

Junctions are signed with trail names. Three trails make the loop: Purisima Creek, Craig Britton, Harkins Ridge.

Go through the gate onto the Purisima Creek Trail; the toilet is just inside. The Harkins Ridge Trail, which also starts here, is your way back.

Look for the huge stumps, some with younger trees growing in a ring round them. Those were the forest; nearly everything standing has grown since. Open flats beside the creek are where mills stood.
21.9 km190 m Grabtown Gulch. A trail leaves for Grabtown Gulch; stay on the creek road. The gulch is named for a lumber camp of the 1880s, and two mills, one cutting shingles, worked at this junction around 1900. From here the road steepens.
33.8 km310 m Craig Britton Trail. Turn left onto this narrow footpath, signed for hikers only. Its first stretch, round a wet, ferny ravine, has the finest redwoods of the day. The trail is named for the man who ran the open-space district from 1994 to 2008.
45.7 km315 m Soda Gulch. The path contours along a steep canyon wall, crosses the gulch, and after about 3 km leaves the redwoods for oak woodland and then open, scented scrub. The last {{u:800 m|half mile}} climbs 75 m in the sun, with the first views out to sea.
57.7 km467 m Harkins Ridge. The high point. Turn left, downhill, on the Harkins Ridge Trail (right climbs to Skyline). The descent is wide, steep and shared with bikes and horses, through firs hung with lichen and banks of blue-flowered ceanothus, with the coast ahead whenever the trees open.

Plan B: start from Skyline · 15.2 km, 730 m of climbing

If the lower car park is full, drive up to the North Ridge car park on Skyline Boulevard (41 spaces, toilet; page 5). Walk down the North Ridge and Harkins Ridge trails 2 km to 5, do the loop from there in the same order, and climb back out. It adds 4 km, and the last 2 km climb 150 m when you are tired: a sign at the top warns that people have needed rescuing. Take more water.

Shorter: the creek and back · any length

The first 1.6 km of the creek trail are nearly level and hold some of the best of the forest. Walk up as far as you like, to Grabtown Gulch (3.8 km return) or to the Craig Britton junction (7.5 km return, 180 m of climbing), and come back the same way.

Field notes

A canyon cut down, and what grew back

The cutting. In 1854, five years after the Gold Rush began, the first sawmill went up at the mouth of this canyon. San Francisco was being built of wood, and redwood splits cleanly into shingles. Over the next seventy years at least seven mills worked along the creek. Loggers stood on planks wedged into notches in the trunk to cut above the swollen base; oxen, and later steam winches, dragged the logs down greased skid roads to the mills. One mill owner, Purdy Pharis, was known as the Shingle King. The pay was a dollar a day and board.

By the early 1900s the trees that were easy to reach were gone, and the last mill closed about 1920. What you walk up is the loggers’ road.

The growing back. A redwood does not die when it is felled. It sprouts from the roots, which is why you see young trees in rings round the old stumps. A century on, those sprouts are tall trees: slender, close together, all of an age. The originals may have been a thousand years old. Redwoods get much of their summer water from fog, which condenses on the needles and drips to the ground; that is why they keep to the wet side of these hills and to the canyons.

How it was kept. Selective logging went on in parts of these hills into the 1970s. The preserve began in the 1980s with a $2 million gift from the Save the Redwoods League to the open-space district, and has grown since to 5,412 acres (2,190 hectares).

Who lives here now. Banana slugs, bright yellow and as long as your hand, cross the trails in damp weather. From November to March newts walk to their breeding pools: do not pick them up, their skin is poisonous. The creek has trout but no salmon or steelhead, because a waterfall where it meets the sea has always kept them out. And in 2022 biologists again recorded marbled murrelets here in numbers: a threatened seabird that feeds on the ocean and nests on the mossy upper branches of big old conifers. Jays and ravens drawn by food scraps raid those nests, so carry every crumb out.

The number of mills is given as six, seven or eight in different accounts, and Pharis’s output as 70,000 or 100,000 shingles a day. The fog figure comes from a study of redwoods further north, not from this canyon.

The name

The creek has been called after la Purísima Concepción, the Immaculate Conception, since Spanish times. A town called Purissima stood near its mouth from the 1850s; it was deserted by the 1930s, and little but its cemetery is left.

Practical

  • Water and toilets: a toilet just inside the gate; no water anywhere. Bring {{u:1.5 litres|a quart and a half (1.5 liters)}} each.
  • Shared trails: the creek and ridge roads are open to bikes and horses. Bikes come down Harkins Ridge fast; keep right. The Craig Britton Trail is for walkers only.
  • Layers: the canyon floor is cold and damp even when Half Moon Bay is sunny, and the ridge can be hot an hour later.
  • Watch for poison oak (glossy leaves in threes) beside the sunny upper trails, and ticks; check yourself afterwards. Rattlesnakes sun on the ridge in warm weather. Mountain lions live here and are very rarely seen.
  • Emergency: 911. Midpen office: +1 650 691 1200.

Getting to the lower trailhead

{{ACCESS}}

Driving route from the Highway 92 / Highway 1 junction: 9.7 km. Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

Half Moon Bay is about 45 minutes from San Francisco by Highway 1, or by Interstate 280 and Highway 92 over the hill. Highway 92 jams on summer weekends and in October, when the pumpkin farms are busy: come early.

AFrom the junction of Highway 92 and Highway 1, go south through town on Main Street for 2.4 km (1.5 mi).
BTurn left onto Higgins Canyon Road. You pass the white Johnston House of 1855 almost at once.
PFollow the road 7.1 km (4.4 mi) up the canyon to the trailhead, on the left just past a small white bridge. The road is narrow and winding, with blind bends and cyclists, but any car should be fine. Seven spaces, plus a few on the roadside where signs allow; parking restrictions are enforced. Free.

Plan B: the Skyline car park

Take Highway 92 east to the summit, turn south on Skyline Boulevard (Highway 35) and go 7 km (4.4 mi) to the North Ridge car park: 41 spaces and a toilet. It also fills on fine weekends.

Without a car

SamTrans buses reach Half Moon Bay, but nothing goes up the canyon, and the trailhead is 7 km from town.
