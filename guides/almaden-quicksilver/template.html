A half-day hike · Almaden Quicksilver County Park, San José

The ghost towns
of Mine Hill

Climb a shaded gulch to a hill where two mining towns stood for half a century, and walk through what is left of them: a few tin-roofed sheds, a flagpole, a furnace rusting in the grass, one tunnel you can still step into. Their mine supplied the mercury the Gold Rush ran on. Some of it is still in the creek.

Why this hike

Most people who come here walk up the wide mine road, admire the view and turn round. This loop instead goes looking for the people: the towns that stood on this hill from the 1850s, and the mine that built and then emptied them.

  • It was the first mine in California, working three years before gold was found, and for decades the most productive mercury mine in North America.
  • Two vanished towns. English Camp, where Cornish miners lived, still has buildings standing. Spanishtown, older and larger, is a name on a sign and a fenced cemetery.
  • And what they worked. A brick powder house, a trestle, a furnace, and a tunnel you can walk into.
  • The story is not finished. The signs at the trailhead telling you not to eat the fish are part of it (page 4).

Start or finish at the mining museum in Casa Grande, {{u:700 m|half a mile}} before the trailhead (weekend afternoons; page 5).

Is this for me?

A half day that most regular walkers can do. The work is in the first hour: 230 m of steady climbing up Deep Gulch.

Overall effortLike a brisk 14 km walk on the flat: about three hours of walking, plus stops at the ruins.
The climbDeep Gulch gains 230 m in 1.9 km, an average 11% with short ramps of over 20%: 40–50 minutes, under oak. Another 100 m of gentle climbing takes you over the top.
UnderfootOld mine roads throughout: wide, firm, stony in the gulch, dusty in summer. No scrambling.
SunThe second half, down the Mine Hill Trail, has little shade. In summer start by 8:00 and carry {{u:1.5 litres|a quart and a half (1.5 liters)}} each.

Read this first

This is an old mercury mine, and it is treated as one. Stay on the trails: about a hundred shafts and tunnels were sealed when the park was made, and not every hole is obvious. Do not climb on the ruins or go past fences. Do not drink from the creeks, springs or ponds, even through a filter: mine waste still lies in the gulches, and a hiking filter does not take out mercury. Carry all your water. Eat nothing caught in the creeks or reservoirs either; the state advises that no fish from them be eaten at all. Walking the trails is considered safe.

The hill in six dates

1845Andrés Castillero, a Mexican army captain, is shown a cave of red rock by Ohlone people who use it for paint. He recognises mercury ore and files a claim.
1850The Main Tunnel is driven into the hill. Mexican and Chilean miners carry ore out on their backs; a town, Spanishtown, grows round the entrance.
1863The Supreme Court rules against the owners. President Lincoln orders the mine seized, armed miners meet his agent at the gate, and he backs down.
1870James Randol takes over as manager and sinks deep shafts. Cornish miners fill English Camp; the mine is at its peak.
1912The ore is poorer and gold mining has moved on. The company goes bankrupt and the towns on the hill empty.
1976After a wartime revival and thirty more years of small-scale mining, the furnace is cold and the county opens the first of the park. The clean-up starts in 1990.

Step by step, and what happened there

Junctions are signed with trail names, and most of the sites have an information board. The red tags give the date each place belongs to.

10.0 km146 m 1850s–1916Hacienda entrance. You are standing in the works. Ore came down from the hill to furnaces here beside Alamitos Creek, was roasted until the mercury boiled off, and left as liquid metal in iron flasks; the brick chimney on the slope above is said to have carried the fumes away from the village. The last furnace here closed in 1916, and what remained of the roasted waste now lies under a cap of clean soil. Look at the outdoor display of mine machinery, then find the Deep Gulch Trail at the overflow car park and start uphill.
21.9 km376 m Top of Deep Gulch. The trail is an old mine road up the west side of the gulch, steady and steep under oaks: the hard part of the day. Heaps of broken rock beside it are mine waste. At the top turn left onto the English Camp Trail.
32.4 km427 m 1860s–1909English Camp. Cornish miners settled here in the 1860s. At its height about a thousand people lived on this shoulder of the hill, with a company store, a Methodist church and a school that had 250 pupils in 1886. By 1909 it was a ghost town. In the 1930s young men of the Civilian Conservation Corps were housed here and cleared away most of what was left; a few sheds, a barn, a flagpole and their memorial remain. On Church Hill, a few minutes up a side path, are the brick chimney and vault of the mine office. Leave on the Castillero Trail, climbing south-west.
43.0 km481 m 1845 · 1940–76The top of the hill. Two ends of the story meet here. The red cave that Castillero was shown, and the Main Tunnel driven from it in 1850, were on this hill (both are now filled in); Spanishtown, the first and largest settlement, lay just below to the south-east (the Hidalgo Cemetery Trail, on your left, leads to the site). A century later the mine’s last operators stripped the hilltop in open cuts and roasted the ore on the spot in the rotary furnace, the fenced tangle of rusted pipe and timber beside the trail. Keep right on the Castillero Trail.
54.1 km483 m Bull Run. The trail crosses the highest ground of the loop (503 m) just below the summit and drops to a junction with picnic tables. Turn right onto the Mine Hill Trail, heading east and gently downhill. It is downhill from here to the car.
64.7 km450 m 1866San Cristobal Mine. A short signed spur leads to the only tunnel on the upper hill that was not sealed. You can walk a little way in, to a barred gate: rails underfoot, cold air, and bats beyond. The block of granite outside was used for miners’ drilling contests.
75.9 km370 m 1866April Trail. Turn left off the mine road onto this loop. It passes the remains of the trestle on which ore cars ran out of the April Tunnel to a chute, and the small brick powder house of 1866, built well away from everything else: in 1875 alone the mine used over {{u:30 tonnes|68,000 pounds}} of blasting powder. The trail rejoins the Mine Hill Trail near English Camp; keep on down it.
87.8 km308 m 1870sCapehorn Pass. A junction with a picnic table. Optional, 1.4 km return: go ahead on the Randol Trail to the Day Tunnel, a sealed opening in a rock face. Driven low on the hill to drain and ventilate the workings above, it became the way most ore came out in the mine’s best years. Otherwise turn right and follow the Mine Hill Trail down, with the Santa Clara Valley opening ahead.

Field notes

Quicksilver: what it was for, who dug it, and what it left

Why here. Along this ridge, bands of serpentine, the slick grey-green rock you cross on the upper trails, were squeezed up into older rocks. Hot fluids later turned their edges into a hard, rusty rock rich in silica and carbonate, and left in it veins of cinnabar, mercury sulphide: a heavy, brick-red ore that glitters in a fresh break. Roast it and the mercury boils off; cool the fumes and it runs out as liquid metal. The earliest ore here was up to a fifth mercury by weight.

What it was for. Mercury dissolves fine gold and silver out of crushed rock and gravel, then gives them back when heated. When gold was found in 1848, the only mercury came by ship from Spain. New Almaden, named after the Spanish mine, roughly halved the price and by 1851 produced nearly half the world’s supply. The Gold Rush, and the silver mines of Nevada after it, ran on flasks from this hill: more than a million of them, {{u:34 kg|76 pounds}} each, by the time it closed. About one twentieth of all the mercury ever mined came from here.

Who dug it. At the peak some 2,500 people worked here, over a thousand underground, on ten- and eleven-hour shifts by candlelight, in workings that reached about 700 m below the summit. The hill was divided: Mexican and Chilean miners in Spanishtown did the most dangerous work for the lowest pay, and struck five times in 1864–65; the Cornish of English Camp got the better jobs. Before ventilation and rules were brought in, mercury poisoning killed about four workers a year.

{{PHOTO:town}}

Lincoln and the mine. The owners’ title rested on Castillero’s Mexican claim. In 1863, in the middle of the Civil War, the Supreme Court threw it out and Lincoln signed an order to take the mine for the government. His agent arrived to find the gate held by armed miners, and within days Washington told him to stand down. The owners sold to a New York company for $1.75 million.

What it left. The roasted waste rock was tipped into the gulches and creeks for a century, and some of it was used to surface the mine roads that are now trails. Mercury from it still washes down Alamitos Creek and the Guadalupe River into San Francisco Bay, about 90 kg a year by the water board’s reckoning, and fish in the reservoirs below the hill carry more mercury than any others in California. Since 1990 the county has dug out the worst of the waste and buried it in one of the open cuts on Mine Hill.

Sources differ on several figures: total output is given as between 1.05 and 1.14 million flasks, the tunnels as 54 or “over 100” miles, the last mining as any year from 1970 to 1976. The Lincoln episode is told here from secondary accounts. The story that Ohlone people fell ill from the red paint comes from one source.

The museum and the village

{{PHOTO:casa}}

Casa Grande (21350 Almaden Road), built in 1854 for the mine’s manager, holds the Almaden Quicksilver Mining Museum: flasking equipment, miners’ lamps, tunnel maps, a model of the furnace you saw on the hill, and Castillero’s iron try-pot of 1845. Open Saturday and Sunday, noon to 4 pm (Fridays for booked groups only); the county suggests a $2 donation. The grounds, with toilets and a drinking-water station, are open daily from 8:00 to sunset.

.

{{PHOTO:valley}}

New Almaden itself is one street of miners’ and managers’ houses along the creek, a National Historic Landmark since 1961. Look for the adobe of about 1850 built by Mexican miners, and on Bertram Road the small Hacienda Cemetery, where one grave holds only an arm, lost by its owner in a hunting accident in 1898.

Shorter: English Camp only, about 5 km

Climb Deep Gulch to English Camp as above, then come straight down the English Camp Trail, which leaves the camp heading east and reaches the Mine Hill Trail a few minutes above the car park. You miss the furnace and the tunnel but get the town, in under two hours.

Getting to the Hacienda entrance

{{ACCESS}}

Driving route from Highway 85: 11.5 km. Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

21785 Almaden Road, San José, CA 95120. About 25 minutes from central San José and 1¼ hours from San Francisco. Paved all the way: any car should be fine.

AFrom Highway 85 in south San José take the Almaden Expressway exit and go south for 6.9 km (4.3 mi).
BTurn right onto Almaden Road and follow it 4.7 km (2.9 mi) up the creek into New Almaden.
PIn the village you pass Casa Grande, the museum, on your left. The Hacienda entrance is {{u:700 m|half a mile}} further, on the right: a large unpaved car park and an overflow area. Free. It is popular with horse riders and can fill on fine weekend mornings; I found no reliable account of how early.

If it is full

The Mockingbird Hill entrance, off Almaden Road before the village, has a large car park, toilets and water, and reaches Capehorn Pass in about 2 km by the Hacienda and Capehorn Pass trails. Join the loop there and walk it in the same order.

Without a car

I found no public transport to New Almaden. If you come by taxi or ride-share, arrange the return before you start.

Practical

  • Hours and fees: the park is open from 8:00 to sunset all year, and free. Trails can close after heavy rain: trail hotline +1 408 355 2200, then 35.
  • Water and toilets: the trailhead has toilets, but the county lists no drinking water there. Fill up at the water station at Casa Grande on the way in. There is none on the trails, and the creeks are not for drinking (page 1).
  • When: November to May for green hills and cool air; spring for flowers. In summer the hill is hot and brown by mid-morning.
  • Shared trails: the Mine Hill Trail is open to bikes and horses, and bikes come down it fast. Keep right and listen.
  • Dogs are allowed on a {{u:lead|leash}}, but not in the museum or its grounds.

Watch for

  • Poison oak (glossy leaves in threes) beside the gulch trail; rattlesnakes on warm days; ticks in grass. Check yourself afterwards.
  • Old workings: stay on the trail and outside every fence, as on page 1.
  • Emergency: 911. Park office: +1 408 535 4070.

Afterwards

New Almaden has no café. Its one restaurant, La Forêt (21747 Bertram Road), is formal French dining in the miners’ boarding house of 1848, dinner only: book ahead and change your shoes. For anything casual, bring a picnic for the tables at English Camp or Bull Run, or drive back to the shops along the Almaden Expressway.
