A half-day hike · Tahoe National Forest, northern Sierra Nevada, California

The stairs to the Sierra Buttes lookout

Climb a forested ridge for an hour and a half, then three flights of open steel stairs bolted to the summit rock, to a fire lookout with a drop on every side. Dinner is beside a lake at the foot of the same cliffs, if you booked a table.

{{PHOTO:hero}}

Why this hike

Most Sierra summits with a view like this cost a full day and a start before dawn. The Sierra Buttes are the highest ground for {{u:28 km|18 miles}} in any direction, and a paved road climbs to within {{u:3.7 km|2.3 miles}} of the top.

  • The stairs. The lookout sits on a crag with no easy way up, so in 1964 Forest Service staff bolted a steel staircase to the rock for visitors: about 180 open steps with the mountain falling away beneath them.
  • The view. From the catwalk you look straight down on the Sardine Lakes, across the Lakes Basin, and on a clear day to Lassen Peak, {{u:105 km|65 miles}} north-west.
  • The evening. Sardine Lake Resort serves dinner on the water under the north-east face, and its bar sits in a gazebo over the lake. Sierra City, the gold-rush town you look down on from the top, is a quarter of an hour further.

It is a morning’s hike. The rest of the day is a swim, a drink over the lake and a table at six (page 4).

Is this for me? Honestly

A short, steady climb that most fit people finish in a morning. The real question is the last five minutes: see-through steel stairs with a lot of air under them, and a grated catwalk over a long drop.

Fitness480 m of climbing in 3.7 km, with no flat stretch to rest on, between 2,135 m and 2,600 m. If you run or cycle regularly you have the engine; coming from sea level that morning, you will breathe harder than you expect.
The stairsThree flights with handrails on both sides. Nothing to scramble, but the treads are open grating and you see the cliff through them. Locals describe gripping both rails all the way down. If heights freeze you, stop at the saddle below the stairs: most of the view is there too.
DogsFine on the trail. The stairs are another matter: a local writer tells of a retriever that trotted up, refused to come down, and had to be carried. Leave the dog with someone at the foot.
Longer optionFrom Sardine Lake, past the Tamarack Lakes: about 16.5 km there and back with 900 m of climbing, and it ends at the bar (page 4).

Read this first

The lookout is a steel building on a steel staircase on the highest rock for miles: keep off it in thunderstorms. Storms build on summer afternoons, so be on top before noon. Turn back if you hear thunder, if clouds are towering by late morning, or if the stairs carry snow or ice. Come down the way you went up. In February 2022 a hiker who took a different line down from the lookout died in a fall from the ridge above Young America Lake.

Step by step

{{u:km|miles}} · heightWhat to do, and what to look for
10.0 km2,135 m The trailhead. A dirt car park beside a green gate, with a notice board, on land owned by a local land trust. The trail goes round the gate and south along the crest of the ridge on an old track. It climbs from the start and does not stop: set a pace you can hold for an hour and a half. In early summer the open stretches are full of wildflowers.
21.4 km2,260 m The Pacific Crest Trail. The long-distance trail from Mexico to Canada crosses the ridge here. Keep straight on, up the ridge, following the signs for the lookout.
31.9 km2,315 m The overlook. Where the trail bends, a short path leads left to the edge of the cliffs. Young America Lake lies directly below, and across it you see for the first time how steep the mountain’s other side is. Keep back from the edge. From here the trail steepens and zigzags up through a shaded stand of big trees, where snow patches linger into early summer and can hide the path.
43.0 km2,495 m The jeep road. The trail comes out on a rough, washed-out vehicle track. Turn uphill. Off-road vehicles use it at times, so you may meet one. The forest ends and the track swings round to a saddle, with the lookout and its staircase in full view on the crag ahead. This is the place to stop if the stairs are not for you, and the place to look at the sky before you commit (page 1). Yellow-bellied marmots live in these rocks.
53.7 km{{u:2,617 m|8,587 ft}} The stairs and the lookout. The track ends at a sign giving the height. Three flights of steel stairs climb the back of the crag, about 180 steps in all, with rails on both sides and open grating underfoot. They top out on a small rock platform just below the cabin, and a last few steps reach the catwalk that runs round it. Hikers report the cabin locked. The catwalk floor is grating too, and at one corner it hangs clear of the rock over a drop of about {{u:90 m|300 ft}}. The table on page 2 says what you are looking at.
17.4 km2,135 m Back the same way. Go down the stairs one person per flight if it is busy, with a hand on each rail. Leave the jeep road where you joined it, at 4: the track itself carries on down the far side of the mountain. Keep to the trail all the way (page 1). The descent takes an hour to an hour and a quarter.

An old volcano above a gold town

Lava from the sea floor. The dark, jagged rock of the Buttes began as lava and ash erupted under the sea from a chain of volcanic islands, about 365 million years ago. Geologists named the rock after this mountain: the Sierra Buttes Formation. Heat and pressure have since altered it into the hard, dark stone that gives the summit its teeth.

Ice on one side. The north-east face is the work of ice-age glaciers, which quarried it into the cliffs under the catwalk. Young America Lake and the Sardine Lakes lie in the hollows the ice left. The side you climb is a forested ramp; the side you look down is a wall.

Gold underneath. The same mountain made Sierra City. The Sierra Buttes Mine worked the south slope, and in 1869 its miners brought out the Monumental nugget, 106 pounds (48 kg) of gold, the largest ever found in the county. Old mine roads still scar the slope below the lookout. The Kentucky Mine museum on the edge of town keeps a working stamp mill and a replica of the nugget; it opens in summer; phone +1 530 862 1310.

The lookout. A fire lookout has stood on the summit since 1915. The present cabin dates from 1963 and the stairs from the year after.

When to go

  • The road decides. Gold Lake Highway and Packer Lake Road are left under snow all winter. The county usually has them clear by the end of May; after a heavy winter it is later, and in June 2023 snow still blocked the road above Packer Saddle. Road conditions: Yuba River Ranger District, +1 530 265 4531.
  • July to early October is the season, and it matches the resort’s (page 4). In June expect snow patches in the forest above 3 and possibly on the stairs.
  • Start by 8:00. The car park holds about twenty cars. One hiker who set off at 7:30 had the top to himself and passed forty people on the way down. An early start also puts you off the summit before afternoon storms.
  • Clear days after a cold front give the longest views. Smoke from wildfires can hide everything in late summer: look at the sky from Bassetts before driving up.

Bring

  • 2 litres of water each. There is none at the trailhead and none on the trail.
  • Sun hat, sunscreen and sunglasses: the top third is in the open.
  • A windproof layer. The catwalk is the windiest place on the mountain.
  • Shoes with grip, for the loose rock on the jeep road.
  • The GPX track or a paper map. Do not count on a phone signal.
  • Swimming things, left in the car for Sand Pond afterwards.

Rules and fees

  • No fee and no permit for a day hike. Parking is free.
  • Dogs are allowed on the trail {{u:on a lead|on a leash}}. Carry out all rubbish.
  • The trailhead has no water. Sand Pond picnic site, by the fork on the way up, has a toilet.

The long way, from Sardine Lake A B

If the hike is meant to end at the resort, it can start there. The Tamarack Connection trail leaves Sardine Lake Road opposite Sardine Campground A, at 1,840 m. It climbs across the hillside north of the Sardine Lakes, with the Buttes on the far side of the water, to the two small Tamarack Lakes B after 4.5 km. From the lakes a jeep track and then a path climb to the ridge, 6.0 km and 540 m of climbing from the car, and join the main route at 2. The lookout is 2.3 km further. In all: about 16.5 km there and back, 900 m of climb, 6–8 hours. It is less walked and less well signed than the main trail, and parts of it are open to off-road vehicles: load the GPX track before you go.

Afterwards: dinner under the mountain

You are down by early afternoon with the best part of the day left. Drive ten minutes back to the fork and take the other road.

  • Swim first. Sand Pond, just below Lower Sardine Lake, is a Forest Service picnic site with toilets and a sandy shore that shelves gently into the water.
  • A drink at five. The bar at Sardine Lake Resort is a gazebo on the lake, under the cliffs you stood on. It opens to everyone at 5 pm and needs no booking.
  • Dinner at six or eight. The resort’s dining room has two {{u:sittings|seatings}}, Wednesday to Sunday, in a lodge the same family has run since 1941. Reservations are required and taken only by phone: +1 530 862 1196. Tables go early in summer, so phone before you fix the date of the hike. Open late May to mid-October (28 May to 16 October in 2027).
  • Without a booking: the restaurant at Sierra Pines Resort in Sierra City serves dinner from 5 to 8 pm daily, May to October, including trout from its own pond.
  • Straight off the hill: Bassetts Station, at the Highway 49 junction, does burgers, milkshakes and pie until 4 pm; closed Wednesdays.

Getting to the trailhead

{{ACCESS}}

Solid blue: from Sierraville, 39 km (24 miles). Dashes: Highway 49 from Downieville to Bassetts, 28 km (18 miles). Basemap: USGS The National Map. Routes: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

Everything hangs off Highway 49. From Interstate 80 at Truckee, take Highway 89 north to A Sierraville and turn west; allow an hour and a half from Truckee to the trailhead. From the west, Highway 49 follows the North Yuba River up from Nevada City through V Downieville and S Sierra City. The road is paved to within {{u:300 m|a quarter of a mile}} of the car park and the last stretch is good gravel: any car should be fine.

BAt Bassetts, 29 km (18 mi) from Sierraville and 8 km (5 mi) above Sierra City, turn north onto Gold Lake Highway.
CAfter 2.1 km (1.3 mi) turn left, signed for Sardine Lake and Packer Lake. In {{u:500 m|a third of a mile}} the road forks: keep right for Packer Lake. Left goes to Sand Pond and Sardine Lake Resort, where you will be this evening.
D4.2 km (2.6 mi) on, at the turning for Packer Lake Lodge, keep left on the paved road signed for the Sierra Buttes. It becomes Forest Road 93: narrow, steep and winding for 3.1 km (1.9 mi) up to Packer Saddle.
PAt the saddle, with signs for the Pacific Crest Trail and the lookout, turn left onto gravel Forest Road 93-2. The car park is on the left after {{u:300 m|a quarter of a mile}}, beside a green gate.

Parking

Free, on dirt, with room for about twenty cars, so on a summer weekend come early (page 4). Keep food out of sight in the car: bears live here.

Staying nearby

The trailhead is about four and a half hours’ drive from San Francisco, so most people stay a night. Sierra Pines Resort in Sierra City and Bassetts Station have rooms; the Forest Service campgrounds nearby (Sardine, Packsaddle, Berger, Diablo) open for the summer. Cabins at Sardine Lake Resort are rented by the week.

Who lives here

Yellow-bellied marmots, the size of a large cat, sun themselves on the rocks near the top and whistle when you come close. Mule deer and black bears use the forest lower down; both are recorded here often, and a bear seen on this trail will almost always leave before you do. Chipmunks and the small, loud Douglas’s squirrel work the cones along the ridge.

The birds you will hear before you see them: Steller’s jays and mountain chickadees in the trees, and fox sparrows and green-tailed towhees scratching in the brush on the open slopes. At the Sardine Lakes afterwards, watch for ospreys and bald eagles fishing, and for river otters, which have been recorded in these lakes several times.
