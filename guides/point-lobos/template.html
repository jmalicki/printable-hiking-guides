A half-day hike · Point Lobos State Natural Reserve, Carmel, California

The otter coves of Point Lobos

Walk in from the gate and follow the edge of the land all the way round: a dozen coves of clear green water floored with kelp, and in the kelp, most days, sea otters asleep on their backs. Seals lie on the rocks below the path, sea lions bark from the rocks offshore, and the cypresses on the headland grow wild in only one other place on Earth. You share it with a great many people.

Why this hike

Most visitors drive in, wait for one of 150 parking spaces, look at one cove and drive to the next. The reserve is small enough to walk round in a morning, and on foot you see every cove in turn, with the road out of sight for most of the way. You can also leave the car on the highway and skip the queue.

  • Sea otters. Volunteers count them here every month: between 42 and 90 adults, and up to 16 pups, on the winter counts of 2019 and 2020. Five stretches of this loop look down on water where they rest and feed (map, page 2; how to find one, page 4).
  • Seals, sea lions and whales. Harbour seals on the rocks all year and pups on the beaches in April; sea lions on the outer rocks; grey whales past the point from December to May (page 5).
  • The cypress grove. Monterey cypresses are planted all over the world and grow wild in two groves. You walk through one of them.
  • The water. On a sunny day the coves are a green you would expect in the tropics, and clear enough to see the kelp to its roots.

Is this for me?

Yes, if you can walk for three hours. It is nearly level, and you are never more than twenty minutes from a road.

Overall effortLike a 10 km walk on the flat. The climbing comes {{u:5–20 m|15–60 ft}} at a time, as the path goes over one headland after another.
UnderfootFirm dirt paths, level and wide on the south shore. The North Shore Trail, 4 to 5, has tree roots, rock and several flights of steps. Trainers are fine.
EdgesThe path runs along low cliffs behind a wire. Waves break over the rocks below without warning: stay on the land side of the wire.
Shorter optionThe north half only, home by the Lace Lichen Trail: 6.8 km, two hours and a half (blue dashes on the map). It keeps Whalers Cove, the cypresses and Sea Lion Point.
WhenAny month. A calm morning is best for otters. April for seal pups, January to March for otter pups and whales. Summer mornings are often foggy.
BringBinoculars, above all. A windproof layer, even in August.

Where to look for otters

2 to 4 Whalers CoveThe most sheltered water in the reserve. A retired ranger describes mothers with pups just off the rocks beside the car park, close enough to watch without binoculars. Harbour seals lie on the rocks across the cove.
4 to 5 North ShoreThe path looks straight down into Bluefish Cove, Big Dome Cove and Cypress Cove from {{u:20–40 m|60–130 ft}} up. Expect single otters here, feeding.
6 to 7 Headland CoveOtters rest in the kelp between the cypress headland and Sea Lion Point. A volunteer with a telescope is often at the Sea Lion Cove viewpoint and will find one for you.
7 to 8 South ShoreThe trail is a few steps from the water past Sand Hill Cove, Weston Beach and Hidden Beach. This side is out of the north-west wind, so the kelp lies flat and otters are easier to pick out.
9 to 10 China Cove and Pelican PointOne published guide to otter-watching on this coast rates this stretch the best in the reserve. Look in the kelp off Pelican Point and in the channel between the point and Bird Island.

The short loop. From Sea Lion Point 7, follow the Sand Hill Trail round and turn left, back to the car park. The Lace Lichen Trail starts across the road and runs {{u:1.2 km|0.7 mi}} through the pines beside it to the entrance station: 6.8 km in all.

If you parked inside, join the loop at whichever car park you are in, 4, 5 or 9, and walk it the same way round.

Field notes · 1

Finding a sea otter

Your chances are good. Volunteers have counted the reserve’s otters once a month since the 1980s, from this same shoreline. In January, February and March of 2019 and 2020 they found between 42 and 90 adults and between 4 and 16 pups; over the years the Point Lobos Foundation puts the range at 30 to 80. An otter rarely goes more than {{u:a couple of kilometres|a mile}} from shore, and it sleeps, eats and raises its young in the kelp, so all of them are within sight of this path.

How to look. Stop wherever the map has a blue name and scan the brown mats of kelp on the surface, slowly, with binoculars. You are looking for a dark log about {{u:1.2 m|four feet}} long with a pale head at one end and two flippers sticking up at the other. The round floats of bull kelp fool everybody: watch a doubtful shape for half a minute, and an otter will roll, scrub its face or dive. On a calm day they rest in the kelp beds outside the coves, alone or in groups called rafts. When a storm brings wind from the south they move into the sheltered coves. Listen as well: the sharp clack, clack carrying over the water is an otter breaking a shell.

{{PHOTO:o_back}}
Is it an otter?everywhere there is kelp

If it is lying on its back, it is. Sea otters are the only sea mammals that swim and rest belly-up as a habit, forepaws on the chest and hind feet held clear of the water to keep them warm. A harbour seal in the water shows a round, spotted, earless head and sinks straight down. A sea lion is much bigger, brown, swims fast and porpoises. A head grizzled almost white marks an old otter.

{{PHOTO:o_pup}}
Mothers and pupsany month; most from January to March

Pups are born in the water in every month, with a peak from January to March and a smaller one from August to October. Look for a mother on her back with a ball of pale wool on her chest. A newborn weighs {{u:about 2 kg|3 to 5 lb}} and its baby fur holds so much air that it cannot dive for its first two months; when she goes down to feed she leaves it bobbing on the surface, often wrapped in a strand of kelp. She nurses it for five to eight months.

{{PHOTO:o_food}}
The table and the toolsfeeding peaks early and late in the day

An otter eats about a quarter of its own weight every day: crabs, snails, sea urchins, clams, mussels, abalone. It dives for a minute or so, mostly to less than {{u:25 m|80 ft}}, and brings the catch up to eat off its chest. A rock serves as hammer or anvil, and a fold of loose skin under each foreleg works as a pocket for the rock and for spare food. Each otter specialises in a few kinds of prey, which it learned from its mother.

{{PHOTO:o_fur}}
The furwhy they never stop grooming

A seal keeps warm with blubber and an otter has none. It has the densest fur of any animal, {{u:up to 150,000 hairs on every square centimetre|up to a million hairs on every square inch}}, about ten times what is on your whole head, and it stays warm only while that fur holds a layer of air. So an otter spends hours a day rolling, rubbing and blowing into its coat. The rest of the answer is food, burned fast, which is why it eats so much.

{{PHOTO:o_raft}}
Anchored in kelpmidday, on calm water

To sleep, an otter often pulls a strand of kelp across its body so that it stays put while the tide runs. The kelp owes the otters something in return. Sea urchins graze kelp down to bare rock, otters eat urchins, and where otters live the kelp forest stands.

{{PHOTO:o_bixby}}
Nearly goneabout 3,000 today

Some 17,000 sea otters lived on the California coast before the fur trade, which began in the 1740s. By 1911, when hunting was banned by treaty, one group was left in the state, off the Big Sur cliffs {{u:17 km|ten miles}} south of here; a 1915 survey saw 32 animals. Every otter in California descends from that group. A magazine photograph of them in 1938 made them famous. The last full count, in 2019, found 2,962, on about an eighth of their old range. They are still listed as threatened, and bites from white sharks are now the main check on their numbers.

Watching without disturbing. From these trails you are above the otters and they ignore you, which is how it should be. An otter that lifts its head to stare at you, stops eating or dives has been disturbed, and waking a resting otter costs it warmth it has to pay for in food. Stay behind the wires and off the closed beaches. Drones are banned in the reserve. Disturbing a sea otter is a federal offence.

Field notes · 2

Who lives here

{{PHOTO:seal}}
Harbour sealsall year · rocks in Whalers Cove 2, Sea Lion Cove 7, China Cove 9

Grey or brown and spotted, {{u:1.5 m|five feet}} long, lying on low rocks curved like bananas with head and tail up. On land they hump along on their bellies. Pups are born from late March to early May, most of them in April, on the beaches at Whalers Cove and China Cove; the beaches are closed then and you watch from the path above, often beside a volunteer keeping count. In 2025 coyotes took newborn pups and the seals abandoned China Cove beach halfway through the season.

{{PHOTO:sealion}}
California sea lionsall year, fewer in summer · Sea Lion Rocks, from 7

You will hear them before you see them, from most of the reserve. The Spanish called the place the point of the sea wolves after that noise. They are brown, up to {{u:2.4 m|eight feet}} long, with small ear flaps, and they walk on their flippers and climb high up the rocks, which a seal cannot do.

{{PHOTO:whale}}
Grey whaleslate December to May · any view of open sea; best from 7

The whole population swims past twice a year, between Alaska and the lagoons of Baja California: south from late December to early February, north from late March to May, the mothers with calves last and often very close to shore. Look for the blow, a low bushy puff, then a mottled grey back with no fin. Humpbacks feed offshore in summer and autumn.

{{PHOTO:cormorant}}
Cormorants and pelicansBird Island, from Pelican Point · nesting in spring and summer

Brandt’s cormorants nest on Bird Island in their hundreds: black, upright birds, each on a mound of seaweed, the males showing a bright blue throat. Western gulls nest among them. The big birds flying past in lines, low over the swells, are brown pelicans, most numerous from summer to autumn.

{{PHOTO:oyster}}
On the rocks and in the pinesall year

The crow-sized black bird with a long orange-red bill and a piercing whistle is the black oystercatcher, which lives its whole life on wave-washed rock. Great blue herons nest in the pines at Coal Chute Point and stand on the floating kelp to fish. Peregrine falcons hunt along the cliffs. Inland, mule deer and brush rabbits are common and unafraid. Bobcats, coyotes and, rarely, a mountain lion pass through.

Two rocks and a rare tree

{{PHOTO:carmelo}}
Pale rock, layered rocknorth shore 2 to 7 · south shore 7 to 9

The pale rock of the north shore is granodiorite, a kind of granite that cooled deep underground 80 to 100 million years ago; look for the big rectangular crystals in it. For six years from 1854 it was quarried at Whalers Cove and shipped to San Francisco. On the south shore the brown, layered Carmelo Formation, some 60 million years old, lies on top of it. It is the filling of an undersea canyon: avalanches of mud, sand and rounded cobbles that settled in layers, later tilted and planed off by the waves. The cobble beds look like poured concrete.

{{PHOTO:cypress}}
Monterey cypressthe headland, 6

This grove and one at Cypress Point, across Carmel Bay, are the only places the tree grows wild. Salt wind prunes the seaward side and fog waters it through the dry summer. The orange coat on trunks and dead branches is an alga coloured by carotene, the pigment of carrots, and does the tree no harm. The grey-green nets hanging from the pines inland are lace lichen.

Getting to Point Lobos

Highway 1 from Monterey to the reserve gate: 8 km (5 miles). Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

The entrance is on Highway 1, on the ocean side, 5 km (3 mi) south of Carmel.

AFrom Monterey, take Highway 1 south, signed for Carmel and Big Sur.
BRio Road traffic lights, at the south edge of Carmel. The shopping centre on the left has the last food and fuel. Keep straight on.
P5 km (3 mi) after Rio Road, a signed right turn into the reserve.

Parking, fees and the gate

Inside: $10 a car, about 150 spaces in several small car parks. They fill: arrive before 9:30 am or after 3 pm.

On the highway: free, on the shoulder on the ocean side of Highway 1, with room for some 200 cars. The inland side is banned. Park parallel, wheels right off the tarmac, and walk to the gate in single file: traffic passes at speed. On a busy day the nearest space can be {{u:a kilometre or more|most of a mile}} from the gate. People who walk in are not charged. Enter only by the front gate.

Hours: opens at 8 am; in winter start the full loop by 1 pm. In November 2026 it closed at 5 pm, last entry 4:30 pm; later in summer. The reserve shuts in storms and heavy surf: check the State Parks page that morning. A shuttle and reservation system was approved in October 2026 with no start date: check whether it has begun.

Practical

  • Water and toilets at the entrance and at the three car parks on the loop, 4, 5 and 9. Nothing to eat or drink is sold inside. Picnic only at the tables.
  • Weather: cool all year, {{u:10 to 18 °C|50 to 65 °F}} on most days, and windy on the points. Summer fog usually clears by midday.
  • Rules: no dogs, even left in the car. No drones, no smoking, no swimming. Stay on the trail and inside the wires. Take nothing, including shells and pebbles.
  • Poison oak (glossy leaves in threes, red in autumn, bare sticks in winter) grows right beside many of the trails.
  • Binoculars are lent at the Information Station 5, open 9 am to 5 pm when volunteers are there.
  • Reserve office: +1 831 624 4909. Emergency: 911.

Afterwards

  • Rio Grill, in The Crossroads shopping centre at Rio Road B, 5 km (3 mi) back towards Carmel. Californian and Southwestern cooking, a bar, and a terrace. Opens at 11:30 am daily; closes at 8 pm, 9 pm on Friday and Saturday. +1 831 625 5436.
  • Sea Harvest Fish Market & Restaurant, in the same centre: a fish counter with tables. Fish and chips, chowder, cioppino. Daily from 8 am to 8:30 pm. +1 831 626 3626.
