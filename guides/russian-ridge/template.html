A half-day hike · Russian Ridge Open Space Preserve, Santa Cruz Mountains, California

Russian Ridge in bloom

Every April the open crest of the mountains above Silicon Valley turns orange with poppies and blue with lupine, and an easy loop takes you straight through it, with the Pacific on one side and the whole bay on the other. Hawks hang at eye level, newts cross the trail after rain, and the way home is through a grove of oaks older than the state. The price is wind, and no shade.

Why this hike

Few hikes near San Francisco give this much for so little climbing. You park at {{u:686 m|2,250 ft}}, already on top of the mountains, and twenty minutes later you are on a bare summit with the whole horizon. Most visitors turn round there. This loop keeps going: along the crest, down a quiet side ridge, and home through the forest.

  • A full-circle view. From Borel Hill you see the Pacific, the bay from San Jose to San Francisco, and Mount Diablo beyond it, often with fog filling the valleys below you.
  • The flowers people drive up for. In spring, says the agency that owns it, this preserve “explodes with color”. From March to June the ridge flowers in waves, and the trail runs through the middle (page 3).
  • Hawks, coyotes, badgers. The same agency calls this one of the best places in the Bay Area to watch birds of prey, and coyotes patrol the ridge (page 5).
  • Newts and salamanders. Five kinds are recorded here, two of them carrying a poison that can kill a person. In the wet months you meet them on the trail (page 4).
  • The Ancient Oaks. A grove of moss-covered giants, some with trunks {{u:well over a metre|four feet and more}} across.

Is this for me?

Yes, if you can walk briskly for two and a half hours. The short loop halves it.

Overall effortLike a brisk 11 km walk on the flat. Four climbs of 50–100 m each, spread round the loop; none takes more than twenty minutes.
UnderfootOld ranch roads and smooth dirt paths. Ordinary trainers are fine when dry. After rain the clay is slippery and sticks to your shoes.
Sun and windThe first {{u:5 km|3 miles}} are open grassland with no shade at all, on a ridge that catches every wind. Hat, sunscreen and a windproof layer, whatever the forecast in the valley.
Shorter optionBorel Hill and the Ancient Oaks only: 3.9 km, 130 m of climb, about an hour and a quarter (blue dashes on the map).
WhenApril and May for flowers. December to March, on a damp day, for newts. Autumn for the clearest views.

Field notes · 1

The flowers, in the order they arrive

The show runs from February to July and changes cast every few weeks. The first violets and buttercups open in February; the big colour is in April and May; the pink clarkias close the season in the drying grass of June and July. How good a year it is depends on how much rain fell and when; for a current report, look at recent photographs from the preserve on iNaturalist. Midpen, the public agency that owns the preserve, names the Ridge Trail, Borel Hill, Hawk Ridge and Alder Spring as the places to look, which is the first {{u:five kilometres|three miles}} of this loop. In flower season the whole preserve is closed to walking off the trail, and nothing may be picked.

{{PHOTO:violet}}
California golden violetFebruary to May, peak in April · open grass

The first to colour the ridge, and called Johnny-jump-up here. A yellow-orange violet on a long bare stalk, with brown veins at the throat and brown on the back of the two upper petals. With it comes the California buttercup: glossy yellow, with many more and narrower petals than the buttercups of gardens.

{{PHOTO:checker}}
CheckerbloomMarch to May · everywhere in the grass

The flower people photograph most on this ridge. A wild mallow, a small cousin of the hollyhock: five pink petals with fine white veins, several flowers up a knee-high stem. Low down among them in March and April, look for red maids, a magenta flower the size of a shirt button that opens only in sun.

{{PHOTO:poppy}}
California poppyMarch to June, stragglers to November · sunny slopes

The state flower, and with lupine the main colour of the display. Four satiny orange petals above feathery blue-green leaves. It shuts in cold, cloud and wind and at night, so a grey morning on the ridge can look flowerless until the sun comes out.

{{PHOTO:lupine}}
LupinesApril and May · the blue in the photographs

Spikes of blue and white pea-flowers above leaves like an open hand. Several kinds grow here and are hard to tell apart; the small annual sky lupine makes the sheets of blue, and the grey shrubby one beside the trail is silver bush lupine.

{{PHOTO:owl}}
Purple owl’s cloverApril to June · short grass, often with poppies

A magenta bottlebrush a hand high. It belongs with the paintbrushes, and like them it is a part-time thief: its roots tap the roots of the plants around it for water and food.

{{PHOTO:tidy}}
Tidy tipsApril to June, peak in May

A yellow daisy whose every petal looks dipped in white paint. In May it shares the slopes with common madia, another yellow daisy, often with a maroon ring round the centre.

{{PHOTO:blue}}
Blue-eyed grass and the wild hyacinthsApril to June

Blue-eyed grass is a tiny iris: six purple-blue petals round a yellow eye, on leaves like grass blades. The taller blues and violets on bare stalks grow from bulbs: blue dicks (a tight purple head, April), Ithuriel’s spear (a loose spray of blue funnels, May) and harvest brodiaea (violet, low in the dry grass, June).

{{PHOTO:clarkia}}
The late shiftMay to July

As the grass turns gold, pink takes over: farewell-to-spring, a clarkia with satin cups, red at the centre, and the smaller wine-coloured winecup clarkia. With them come two big yellow daisies: mule’s ears, with upright leaves the size of the name, and gumplant, whose buds hold a blob of sticky white resin.

In the shade. The Ancient Oaks Trail has its own flowers, earlier and quieter: blue hound’s tongue from February, then giant trillium (three broad leaves, one dark flower sitting on them) and the brown-and-green bells of checker lily in April, and the nodding white globe lily in May.

Why here? There is no special rock under this ridge. The flowers are here because the grassland is. Wind and thin soil keep trees off the top, cattle kept it open (the Russian of the name was a Mr Paskey, who ran a dairy here from about 1920 to 1950), and Midpen now does that work by mowing, pulling thistles by hand and, between 1998 and 2009, by burning. Most of the grass itself is European, sown or carried in with the cattle; the flowers are the natives that have held on among it.

Field notes · 2

Newts and salamanders: who is under the logs

Five kinds of salamander are recorded in this preserve. You will see none of them on a dry day in summer: they spend it underground or under bark, waiting. They come out with the first soaking rain of autumn and stay active until the ground dries in May. Visitors’ records of newts here run from November to May and peak in March and April, with none at all from June to October. Go on a mild, damp day, walk slowly through the forest between 7 and 8, and look at the trail in front of your feet. Midpen names two places in this preserve where newts cross in numbers: Alpine Road near the foot of the Ancient Oaks Trail, and the Mindego Hill Trail. Look, photograph, and leave them where they are. Do not turn logs over, and do not pick anything up: it is against the rules, and in one case it is bad for you.

{{PHOTO:newt}}
California newtforest and grass, in daylight, on the trail

The one you are most likely to meet, because it walks about in the open by day. {{u:12–20 cm|5–8 in}} long, slow, with dry grainy skin: brown above, yellow-orange beneath, with pale lower eyelids. It lives on land for most of the year and walks to a pond to breed; Midpen gives November to March as the season for these marches. It can live more than twenty years.

{{PHOTO:rough}}
Rough-skinned newtthe same places; look at the eyes

Its near twin, recorded here by visitors about a third as often. The difference is in the face. The California newt has light skin under the eye and eyes that bulge past the line of the jaw when seen from above. The rough-skinned newt is dark under the eye, with eyes set inside the outline of the head, and a sharper line between dark back and orange belly.

{{PHOTO:belly}}
Why nothing eats themand why you keep your hands off

Newt skin holds tetrodotoxin, the nerve poison of the pufferfish. One study put the skin of a single rough-skinned newt at enough to kill 25,000 mice; the California newt is about a tenth as strong. It harms only if it gets inside you, through the mouth, an eye or a cut; Midpen warns that swallowing it can kill a person. A threatened newt arches its back and lifts its head and tail to show the orange: a warning. That is why it can afford to stroll about in daylight.

{{PHOTO:ensatina}}
Ensatina, the impostorunder logs and bark in the forest

Smooth, moist and long-legged, {{u:8–15 cm|3–6 in}}, orange-brown with an orange belly, a yellow patch in the eye, and a tail pinched in at the base, which it can drop. It is harmless, and the colours are borrowed: in a 2008 experiment, scrub-jays that had just been shown a newt took six times as long to touch a yellow-eyed ensatina as a plain-coloured one. It has no lungs. Like the next two it breathes through its skin, and so must stay damp.

{{PHOTO:slender}}
California slender salamanderleaf litter, anywhere there is cover

Looks like an earthworm with a face: {{u:8–14 cm|3–5½ in}} of thin dark body, with four legs so small you need to look twice. It is one of the least travelled animals alive: studies find that most never move more than {{u:two metres|a few feet}} from where they hatched.

{{PHOTO:arboreal}}
Arboreal salamanderthe old oaks; out on wet nights

The salamander of the Ancient Oaks, though with one record here it is the hardest to find. Brown with cream-yellow spots, up to {{u:18 cm|7 in}}, with a broad head, squared toe-tips and a tail that grips. It climbs, and lays its eggs in hollows of live oaks; one was found {{u:18 m|60 ft}} up. It can bite.

{{PHOTO:garter}}
The snake that eats newtsMindego ponds, west of the loop; closed to visitors

One predator has beaten the poison. Garter snakes on this coast have evolved resistance to it, and eat newts whole. The most beautiful of them, the San Francisco garter snake, red, black and turquoise, is an endangered species, and a US Geological Survey study counted 100 to 150 of them around the old ranch ponds under Mindego Hill, with red-legged frogs and pond turtles. Those ponds are closed to visitors and well to the west of this route.

Also here, and not here. The small frog you hear after rain, far louder than its size, is the Pacific chorus frog. Two salamanders of these mountains have no record in the preserve: the California giant salamander, {{u:up to 30 cm|a foot}} long and marbled like a brown trout, which lives in cold streams in the neighbouring preserves, and the Santa Cruz black salamander.

Field notes · 3

Everything else: oaks, badgers, hawks and a blue bird

The loop has two halves, and each has its own residents. For the first {{u:five kilometres|three miles}} you are in grassland, where the life is overhead or underground. For the last three you are on the forest edge, and the line between the two is where most animals do their business. Early and late in the day are best for all of them.

{{PHOTO:oaks}}
The ancient oaks7 to 8

Nearly all are canyon live oaks, evergreen, with small leaves that are gold-felted beneath when young. About sixteen are giants, with trunks {{u:60–140 cm|two to four and a half feet}} across, and one has ten trunks. One published estimate puts them at three centuries or more. The green fur is moss and the grey-green rags are lichen, both fed by fog and neither harming the tree. What does harm it is sudden oak death, a water mould that arrived in California in the 1990s. It was here that researchers first found canyon live oaks could catch it, and bay trees, which carry it, were cut from around the big oaks. Do not climb them.

{{PHOTO:grass}}
The grass, and what is coming for it1 to 6

Green from December, gold from June. The tall summer grasses, wild oat and Harding grass, are European; the native is purple needlegrass, growing in separate tufts with long bent bristles. The round grey-green bush dotted across the slopes is coyote brush, the first step in grassland turning to scrub and then forest. The spiny yellow thistle that volunteers pull each summer is yellow starthistle.

{{PHOTO:badger}}
American badgerthe grassland; look for the holes

This ridge is one of Midpen’s two best places for badgers, and the trail north of 4 is named Badger’s Byway. You are unlikely to see one, since they hunt at night, but you can see where they have been: a burrow mouth wider than it is tall, like a letterbox, with a fan of fresh earth. They dig for gophers. Other animals move into the holes afterwards; cameras here have recorded newts and a wintering burrowing owl using them.

{{PHOTO:coyote}}
Coyotes and the other huntersridge and forest edge

The coyote is the one you may well see, trotting the ridge or standing in the grass listening for voles. Bobcats (twice the size of a house cat, short tail), grey foxes and long-tailed weasels are here too. So are mountain lions, seen occasionally. If you meet one: do not run, face it, stand tall, make noise, and back away slowly. In the forest, the waist-high heaps of sticks are the houses of dusky-footed woodrats, which salamanders share.

{{PHOTO:kestrel}}
Hawks at eye level2 to 5; best in spring and autumn

The wind that hits the ridge is pushed upwards, and birds of prey ride it. Red-tailed hawks and turkey vultures (wings held in a shallow V, rocking) are constant. The small falcon hanging in one spot on beating wings is an American kestrel; the white one doing the same is a white-tailed kite. A long-tailed hawk flying low over the grass with a white patch above the tail is a northern harrier. Golden eagles pass through.

{{PHOTO:bunting}}
Lazuli buntinglate April to July; bushes in the grassland

The bird most often recorded here by visitors. The male is turquoise-blue with an orange breast and white belly, and sings from the top of a coyote brush. It winters in Mexico and arrives with the flowers. The grassland also has western bluebirds, meadowlarks and the insect-voiced grasshopper sparrow; coveys of California quail and flocks of wild turkeys work the forest edge.

{{PHOTO:checkerspot}}
ButterfliesApril to June, on the flowers

The commonest by far is the variable checkerspot: black, chequered with cream and red spots. Its spiny black caterpillars feed on sticky monkeyflower and bee plant along the forest edge. The small blue ones over the lupines are silvery blues, whose caterpillars eat lupine and are guarded by ants in exchange for a sweet secretion.

{{PHOTO:lizard}}
Lizards and snakeswarm days; trail edges, rocks, fence posts

The lizard doing push-ups on every post is the western fence lizard. Its blood contains a protein that kills the Lyme disease bacterium inside any tick that feeds on it. The long snakes of the grassland are harmless gopher snakes and racers. The northern Pacific rattlesnake lives here too, has a broad triangular head and usually rattles: step back and walk round it.

Getting to Russian Ridge

{{ACCESS}}

Driving route from Interstate 280: 14 km (8.7 miles). Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

Russian Ridge car park, at the corner of Skyline Boulevard (Highway 35) and Alpine Road. Midpen says to allow 40 minutes from Interstate 280. There is no bus.

ALeave Interstate 280 at exit 20, Page Mill Road, and head west, away from Palo Alto and towards the hills.
Stay on Page Mill Road for 14 km (8.7 mi). It is paved all the way, but it is one narrow lane each way and climbs {{u:600 m|2,000 ft}} in continuous bends. Any car is fine.
PAt the stop sign on the crest, go straight across Skyline Boulevard; the road becomes Alpine Road. The car park is the first turn on the right, {{u:100 m|100 yards}} after the crossroads.

Other ways up. From Highway 84 at Skylonda, drive 12 km (7.3 mi) south on Skyline Boulevard and turn right at the Page Mill crossroads. From Highway 9 at Saratoga Gap, drive 10 km (6.5 mi) north on Skyline and turn left.

Parking

Free, 37 spaces, with a toilet and no drinking water. The preserve is open from half an hour before sunrise to half an hour after sunset. On spring weekends and evenings it can fill: come early, or late in the afternoon when the light is better anyway. The overflow is the Mindego car park, 20 spaces, {{u:2.4 km|1.5 miles}} further down Alpine Road, from which a path climbs to the Ancient Oaks Trail and joins this loop at the east end of the Ancient Oaks Trail. The viewpoint car park on Skyline Boulevard north of the crossroads was closed in November 2026.

Practical

  • Water: none anywhere on the route or at the car park. Carry {{u:a litre|a quart (1 liter)}} each, more on a hot day.
  • Weather: the ridge is cooler and far windier than the valley, and fog can sit on it all day in summer while the bay is in sun. Bring a layer.
  • Daylight: in winter the sun sets around 5 pm. Start by 1:30 pm.
  • Rules: no dogs. Stay on the trail. Take no plants or animals. Cyclists and riders share most of these trails: walkers give way to horses.
  • Poison oak (glossy leaves in threes, red in autumn, bare sticks in winter) lines the forest trails. Check for ticks afterwards.
  • Phone signal comes and goes: load the GPX file before you leave. Emergency: 911. Midpen rangers: +1 650 691 1200.

Afterwards

  • Alice’s Restaurant, at the Highway 84 crossroads, 12 km (7.3 mi) north along Skyline Boulevard. Burgers, breakfasts and pie among redwoods and motorcycles. Open daily from 8 am; closes at 8 pm, 7 pm on Sunday. +1 650 851 0303.
  • Alpine Inn (“Zott’s”), Portola Valley: a roadhouse beer garden near the foot of the hill, by the next Interstate 280 exit north of Page Mill Road. Burgers and beer at outdoor tables; no reservations. Closed Monday. +1 650 854 4004.

Hours change: check on the day.
