The hazardous crossing from Canyon Creek to Sapphire Lake

A hiker asked online whether the granite crest between the Canyon Creek Lakes and Sapphire Lake can be crossed. Backpackers who know the range advised strongly against it, citing a serious risk of injury or death. This guide documents that crossing for reference. Do not attempt it on the strength of these pages.

Extremely hazardous: read this first

This route carries a serious risk of injury or death. The 5 km between L Lake and Mirror Lake have no trail: steep scree, a loose gully and a ledge between cliffs, a full day from help and without phone signal. The route has not been walked or surveyed for this guide; the line on the maps is approximate and cairns come and go. Both lake basins can be reached by maintained trail, each up its own valley. Anyone still considering the crossing should treat page 3 as a briefing, carry a proper topographic map, and first ask at the Weaverville ranger station, +1 530 623 2121.

The road to the finish needs high clearance: the last 3 km to the Stuart Fork trailhead, where you leave the second car, are dirt, and the final hill is not maintained for ordinary cars (page 6). The road to the start is paved.

What this is

Two of the great valleys of the Trinity Alps, Canyon Creek and Stuart Fork, run side by side and end in lake basins barely 2 km apart. Good trails lead up each. Nothing connects them except the ridge between, and a route over it that exists only as cairns, deer tracks and a handful of trip reports.

  • Day 1 is the most popular hike in the range for good reason: waterfalls, meadows and two lakes under the highest peak.
  • Day 2 is the crossing, and the subject of the warning above: from the last lake, over a {{u:2,260 m|7,420 ft}} notch informally called Kalmia Pass, down a ledge between cliffs to Mirror Lake, then to Sapphire and Emerald.
  • Day 3 is a long, easy hike out through meadows and forest, past the ruins of a gold-rush waterworks.

You finish 60 km by road from where you started: see page 6 for the car shuttle.

Is this for me? Honestly

For almost all readers, no. The crossing requires established off-trail experience: reading terrain, judging loose ground, and turning back.

FitnessThree days of 6–8 hours with a {{u:12–15 kg|25–35 lb}} pack. If you have done a multi-day backpacking trip, you are fit enough.
Skill5 km with no path: steep scree, sidehilling on hard dirt, a loose gully, some hands-on down-climbing. Harder than any unmarked pass you have crossed, with a heavy pack.
CommitmentNo phone signal for three days. From Mirror Lake, help is a full day away.
Not forA first backpacking trip, a solo first visit, or anyone uneasy on loose, exposed ground. Page 5 has the alternative.

Planning the crossing day

  • Go in August or the first half of September: dry ledge, long days, low streams.
  • Camp at L Lake on night 1 and be on the pass by 9:00.
  • Carry 3 litres each from L Lake: there is nothing reliable before Mirror Lake, a helmet if you have one, and poles.
  • The crossing can be reversed, but the gully is easier to find from below.

Turn back if

  • there is snow on the north side of the pass (usual until mid-July, sometimes later) and you have no axe and the skill to use it;
  • you reach the pass after about 1 pm: the descent has no good place to stop and Mirror to Sapphire is unwise in the dark;
  • cloud hides the ledge, or anyone in the party is unhappy on the first loose traverse. It does not get easier.
3Upper Canyon Creek Lake to L Lake · 1.6 km, +250 m, 1–1½ h
The trail ends on the east shore of the upper lake (1,745 m). A cairned use path, not on most maps, continues round to the inlet and climbs north-east beside the stream that drains L Lake. It is steep, up to 45% in places, and easy to lose in brush and slabs; keep the stream within earshot.
4L Lake to Kalmia Pass · 1.5 km, +270 m, 1–1½ h Last water for 5–10 hours · fill 3 litres each
L Lake (1,990 m; also written “El” or “Little” Lake) sits in a granite bowl with camping and the last reliable water before Mirror Lake. Cross the outlet, walk through the meadow north of the lake, and climb north-west towards a dry stream bed leading to the lowest notch in the ridge. The reports agree on one thing: the col goes at its lowest point, not elsewhere. Scree and grass, steep but not technical.
5Kalmia Pass · about 2,260 m (7,420 ft), near 40.9880° N, 123.0232° W
Behind you, the Canyon Creek Lakes and Mount Hilton. Ahead, Sapphire Lake 470 m below, a sliver of Emerald, and the serrated wall of Sawtooth Ridge. A spring-fed tarn just below the pass was the only water one party found.
6Pass to Mirror Lake: the crux · about 1.5 km, −245 m, 2 to 5½ h
Do not drop straight down: there are cliffs and unstable talus below. Instead trend left (north-west), descending on grass, talus and deer tracks to a broad sloping ledge sandwiched between two cliff bands. Follow it: it is longer than it looks, a lot of sidehilling, with a couple of washed-out stretches on steep hard-packed dirt where a slip matters. Keep going until an obvious loose gully opens with Mirror Lake directly below. Descend it one at a time, then move left onto granite slabs and benches down to the lake (2,015 m).
In September 2001 a party took about two hours. In late June 2018, on soft steep snow, another took five and a half, was unsure the ramp would go until they reached it, and called the loose traverse the hard part.
6Mirror Lake to Sapphire Lake · 2 km, −220 m, 1½–2 h
Still no trail: faint tread and cairns down granite beside the outlet stream, with a few moves where you face in and use your hands, and brush lower down. At Sapphire Lake (1,795 m) a faint, brushy use path follows the north shore to the outlet; expect to lose it once or twice.
7Sapphire Lake to Emerald Lake · 1.4 km, −110 m, 45 min
A real, if rough, trail at last: cairns over granite rubble to the stone dam at Emerald Lake (1,690 m).

Day 3: down Stuart Fork

{{PHOTO:mirror}}{{PHOTO:relic}}{{PHOTO:dam}}
820.4 km1,690 m (5,550 ft) Emerald Lake. Look at the dam before you leave: fitted granite blocks, with rusted gears, winches and cable lying where they were abandoned. At the turn of the 20th century the La Grange company dammed Emerald and Sapphire to store water for its hydraulic gold mine near Weaverville, and carried it there through some 45 km of ditch, flume and tunnel. It was the largest hydraulic mine in California until it closed in 1918.
about 25 km1,340 m The trail drops through Portuguese Camp to Morris Meadow, {{u:a kilometre|over half a mile}} of open grass with the white wall of Sawtooth Ridge behind you: the classic view of the Trinity Alps, and the best place for a fourth night. Deer at dusk, and often a bear.
about 32.5 km1,080 m Deer Creek bridge, then the Alpine Lake junction. The loop finish (below) turns right here. Otherwise the last 8 km are a wide, easy forest trail high above the river, hot on a summer afternoon.
9{{u:40.2 km|25 mi}}834 m (2,740 ft) Stuart Fork trailhead at Bridge Camp: campground, toilets, drinking water in season, and your second car.

Other ways to finish

Loop back to your car over Bear Creek (blue dashes on the map; no shuttle). From the Alpine Lake junction, ford Stuart Fork, climb the Alpine Lake and Bear Creek trails over a 1,940 m divide and descend to the Canyon Creek trailhead: 24.9 km from Emerald Lake with 860 m of climbing, making 45 km in all and a fourth day. Be warned: the ford is dangerous before about mid-July, and hikers describe the Bear Creek Trail as steep, shadeless, brushy and unpleasant. It works; it is nobody’s favourite.

Return the way you came. Recross the pass on day 3 and walk out Canyon Creek on day 4. The gully is easier to find from below, but you do the loose ground twice.

Three smaller trips, same country

  • Canyon Creek Lakes and back: 25 km return, all on trail. A long day for the fit, a perfect overnight.
  • Add the pass as a day hike. From a camp at the upper lake, climb to L Lake and Kalmia Pass without packs (6 km return, +520 m), look down on Sapphire Lake, and come back. All of the view, none of the crux. This is the version to choose if in doubt.
  • Sapphire Lake by trail: up Stuart Fork from Bridge Camp to Emerald and Sapphire, 43 km return, three days, no route-finding.
Field notes

Why it looks like the Alps

White granite. Most of the Klamath Mountains are dark, crumpled sea-floor rock. Here a body of pale granite pushed up through it, and Ice Age glaciers carved that granite into horns, knife ridges and stepped lake basins. Locals speak of the White Trinities (this granite core), the Red Trinities (rust-coloured serpentine to the east) and the forested Green Trinities to the west. Your route stays in the white.

Ice. Thompson Peak, about 2,740 m and the highest point in the range, held small glaciers on its north side into this century, at a far lower altitude than any in the Sierra Nevada. They have shrunk to remnants. The snow that can block Kalmia Pass in July is the same story: this range catches enormous winter snowfall from the Pacific, less than {{u:100 km|60 miles}} away.

Trees. The Klamath Mountains never fully glaciated and sit where coastal, Cascade and Sierra floras meet, so they hold one of the richest conifer forests on Earth: more than thirty species in the region. On day 1 you climb through Douglas fir, sugar pine and incense cedar into red fir and mountain hemlock. Around the Canyon Creek Lakes look for Brewer spruce, with long, drooping, curtain-like branchlets: an Ice Age relic that grows nowhere outside these mountains.

Animals. Black bears are common and bold in Canyon Creek, hence the canister rule. Rattlesnakes sun themselves on the lower trail; rough-skinned newts and trout fill the lakes.

Rules, season, kit

  • Permit: a free wilderness permit is required to camp. Self-issue at the trailhead board or outside the Weaverville ranger station (360 Main St). No quota.
  • Bear canister: required in the Canyon Creek drainage (Forest Order 14-25-03, in force to September 2028). Rent or buy one before you arrive.
  • Fires: wood fires are banned around the Canyon Creek Lakes and in other marked zones; assume none at any of the lakes. A gas stove needs a free California Campfire Permit (permit.preventwildfiresca.org).
  • Group size 10. Camp and wash 60 m from water. Dogs are allowed.
  • Season: the trails open in June; the crossing is best from late July to mid-September. In June and early July expect steep snow on the pass and high, cold fords. It is under snow from November to May.
  • Kit beyond the usual: paper map and compass as well as a phone with offline maps, sturdy boots, poles, sun protection (the granite glares), a head net in July, a satellite messenger if you can borrow one.
  • Supplies: Weaverville has a supermarket, cafés and motels. Nothing after Junction City.

Water

Where it is

  • Day 1. For the first 4 km the creek is far below the trail; start with a full bottle. Above McKay Camp the trail runs beside it to the lakes. At The Sinks the creek goes underground late in the year (dry in late September 2018).
  • Day 2. Fill up at L Lake. The gully to the pass is a dry stream bed, and the only water any report mentions before Mirror Lake is one small spring-fed pool below the pass (2001). Early in the season there is snow to melt.
  • Day 3. Lakes, then the river and side streams beside the trail all the way down.

How clean, and what to do to it

  • It is clear. Snowmelt off granite: no silt, no colour, no report of algae blooms. A filter, tablets or a UV pen is enough; nothing needs settling first.
  • Treat all of it. The Forest Service says Giardia is present in most of the wilderness, and Canyon Creek takes a third of all its visitors. Draw from inlets and moving water, upstream of tents.
  • Upstream. Pack horses graze Morris Meadow; I found no record of cattle, or of contamination from the old mine works.
  • Burns. The 2023 Deep Fire burned part of the Stuart Fork valley. After a storm, side streams there can run grey: use the river.

Getting there, and the car shuttle

From San Francisco allow 5½–6 hours: Interstate 5 north to Redding (350 km, 3½ h), then west.

ARedding. Leave I-5 for Highway 299 west (signed Eureka / Weaverville), a winding mountain road.
BWeaverville, 70 km and about an hour on: last supplies, fuel and the ranger station. Highway 3 north to the finish trailhead leaves from here.
CContinue 13 km (8 mi) on Highway 299 to Junction City and turn right onto Canyon Creek Road. It is easy to miss: if you pass the shop and fire station you have gone too far.
PFollow Canyon Creek Road 21 km (13 mi) to its end at the Canyon Creek trailhead: paved but narrow, about 30 minutes; any car should be fine. Large free car park, toilet, no water. Ripstein campground is 1.5 km before the end.
FStuart Fork trailhead (finish). From Weaverville take Highway 3 north 21 km (13 mi), turn left on Trinity Alps Road, pass through the old resort and continue 3 km on dirt to Bridge Camp, which has drinking water from late May to 31 October.
High-clearance road · last 3 km of dirtGravel at first, then a low-water crossing and a steep hill of bare dirt for the last 0.7 km. The Forest Service does not maintain that part for ordinary cars and says it may need high clearance and four-wheel drive.

The shuttle

The trailheads are about 60 km (38 mi) and 1¼–1½ hours apart by road, through Weaverville. There is no public transport and no regular shuttle service. Bring two cars and leave one at Bridge Camp on the way in, or use the Bear Creek loop finish (page 5).

Leave nothing in view in either car, and no food at all: this is bear country.

Staying nearby

Weaverville is a well-kept gold-rush town with motels and places to eat. Camp the night before at Ripstein (Canyon Creek Road) to start early, and consider a night at Bridge Camp or the lakeside campgrounds on Trinity Lake afterwards.
