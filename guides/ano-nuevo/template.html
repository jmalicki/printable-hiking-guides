A half-day hike · Año Nuevo State Park, San Mateo coast, California

Out to the elephant seals of Año Nuevo

Walk {{u:three flat kilometres|two flat miles}} across old pasture and a field of dunes to the end of a point, and look down on a beach of animals the size of cars. In winter the bulls fight in the surf and the pups are born on the sand; for the rest of the year the seals come back in shifts to shed their skins. You go on the park’s terms: with a guide in winter, on a free permit otherwise.

Why this hike

At the best-known elephant seal beach in California, further down the coast, you watch from a boardwalk beside the highway. Here the seals are half an hour’s walk from the nearest car, in a field of dunes, and the walk out is part of the day: hawks over the scrub, a pond with one of the rarest snakes in North America, and sea lions barking from an island with a ruined house on it.

  • The seals. The park counts up to 10,000 elephant seals using the point in a year, and about 5,000 in the colony in the winter breeding season. The rule is {{u:8 m|25 feet}}, which is close when the animal weighs two tons.
  • Something in every month except the first two weeks of December: fights and births in winter, weaned pups in spring, moulting seals all summer, youngsters in autumn (the calendar is on page 2).
  • The island. {{u:Less than a kilometre|Half a mile}} off the point, closed to the public and crowded with sea lions, seals and seabirds. You hear it before you see it.
  • It is close. About an hour and a half south of San Francisco, on Highway 1.

Is this for me?

Yes, if you can walk for two and a half hours, part of it on loose sand. Getting in takes more planning than the hike does.

The winter guided walk

From 15 December to 31 March you walk the same trail to the staging area 3 by yourself. Beyond the gate a volunteer naturalist leads a group of up to fifteen, on whatever line the rangers have chosen that day to keep clear of the seals: about {{u:5 km|three miles}} in all, two and a half hours from start to finish. In winter seals lie in the dunes as well as on the beaches. Arrive early; a late arrival loses the place and the money.

More coast, with no permit

Cove Beach, below the pond, is open to sunset all year. Franklin Point, from a free roadside trailhead {{u:6 km|four miles}} north on Highway 1 (F on the map, page 6), is about {{u:1 km|half a mile}} each way through dunes to a rocky point where the clipper Sir John Franklin was wrecked in fog in 1865. It looks north to Pigeon Point lighthouse and south to the island. From there the Atkinson Bluff Trail follows the cliff edge south for {{u:3 km|two miles}}.

Permits, bookings and fees

Parking: $10 a car, paid at the kiosk. Park hours: 8:30 am to sunset.

April to November: the permit is free and needs no booking. It is issued from 8:30 am to 3:30 pm and you must leave the seal area by 5 pm.

15 December to 31 March: $7 a person for the guided walk, plus a $3.99 booking fee; children of four and under go free. Book at reservecalifornia.com or on +1 800 444 4445. The season went on sale on 20 October 2026, each date opening 56 days ahead. Weekends and holidays go first. There are no refunds or exchanges unless the park cancels. No public walks on 25 December or on 23–24 January 2027, a ticketed fundraising weekend.

Field notes · 1

Watching elephant seals

What you are looking at. A beach of elephant seals looks at first like a beach of driftwood logs, until one of them lifts its head. They spend most of the year alone at sea and come ashore for three things only: to give birth, to mate and to moult. On land they fast, so every animal you see is living on its blubber and saving energy. Long stillness is normal. So is the sudden flurry when one bull gets too near another. Watch the edges of a group, where the action starts, and listen: the bulls’ call is a slow, hollow drumming that carries across the whole point.

{{PHOTO:size}}
Bull, cow or pup?all year

A full-grown bull is {{u:4 to 5 m|14 to 16 feet}} long, weighs up to two and a half tons, and has the long hanging nose the animal is named for. His chest is a shield of scarred, pink, thickened skin. A female is {{u:3 to 3.5 m|10 to 12 feet}} long and a third of his weight or less, with a smooth face and no trunk. Young males show a nose that is part-grown. A pup is born black.

{{PHOTO:fight}}
The fightsDecember and January

The bulls arrive first, in December, and settle who outranks whom before the females come. Most meetings end with a threat: head back, nose inflated, a burst of drumming. When neither gives way they rear up chest to chest and slash at each other’s necks with their teeth. The few bulls at the top do most of the mating. A fight can cross a beach quickly, and a bull does not look where he is going.

{{PHOTO:harem}}
Births and nursinglate December to February

Females land from late December and gather in harems. Each gives birth three to six days after arriving, to one pup of about {{u:34 kg|75 lb}}. She nurses for about four weeks on milk that is half fat, eating nothing herself, and the pup reaches {{u:110–160 kg|250–350 lb}}. Then she mates, goes back to sea and leaves it.

{{PHOTO:weaners}}
WeanersMarch and April

The abandoned pups are called weaners. At four to six weeks old they shed the black coat for a silver one and collect in heaps. They learn to swim by themselves, in rain pools and the shallows, wary of the water at first. Through the last weeks of April they leave one at a time and head north. A few that stole milk from a second mother are twice the usual size.

{{PHOTO:moult}}
The moultApril to August

Other mammals shed hair a little at a time. An elephant seal loses the whole outer layer of skin with the fur attached, in sheets, over four to six weeks ashore. The animals look ragged and diseased and are perfectly well. They come in turns: females and juveniles in spring, young males in early summer, the big bulls in July and August.

{{PHOTO:nose}}
At seamost of their lives

An elephant seal at sea is under water nearly all the time. A usual dive lasts twenty minutes and goes to {{u:300–600 m|1,000–2,000 feet}}, after squid, skates, rays, ratfish and small sharks. The deepest on record is {{u:1,761 m|more than a mile}} down. Sharks and killer whales hunt them.

{{PHOTO:dunes}}
Nearly gonemore than 200,000 today

Sealers killed them for the oil in their blubber. By 1892 one small herd was left, on Guadalupe Island off Mexico; sources put it at between fifty and two hundred animals. Mexico protected them in 1922 and the United States soon after. They reached Año Nuevo Island in 1955, the first pup was born there in 1961, bulls landed on the mainland in 1965, and the first mainland pup was born in January 1975.

Keeping your distance. Stay at least {{u:8 m|25 feet}} from every seal, including one that looks dead: it is asleep. A resting seal carries its head tucked back and can throw it forward {{u:nearly a metre|two or three feet}} to bite, and over a short distance on sand it moves faster than you expect. Never stand between a seal and the water or between two bulls. Do not touch a seal, alive or dead: bird flu reached this colony in February 2026.

Field notes · 2

Who lives here

After the elephant seals, visitors’ records for the park are led by sea lions, deer, brush rabbits and brown pelicans. In walking order from the car park:

{{PHOTO:bobcat}}
Rabbits, deer and bobcatsall year · the old pasture, 1 to 3

Brush rabbits feed at the edge of the gravel and bolt into the coyote brush. Black-tailed deer graze the open ground. Both are easy to see early and late in the day. Visitors photograph bobcats here regularly, hunting gophers and voles in the grass: tawny, long-legged, twice the size of a house cat, with a short tail. Coyotes use the same ground. California quail call from the thicket where the New Years Creek Trail leaves.

{{PHOTO:harrier}}
Harriers and kitesall year · over the scrub, 1 to 4

The long-winged hawk quartering low over the brush, tilting from side to side with a white patch at the base of its tail, is a northern harrier. The small white one hanging in the air on beating wings is a white-tailed kite. Red-tailed hawks and turkey vultures circle higher. Peregrine falcons hunt along the cliffs.

{{PHOTO:snake}}
The pondspring to autumn, on warm days · 2

The snake you are likely to see sunning beside the trail is the coast garter snake, dark with a yellow stripe. The pond’s famous resident is the San Francisco garter snake, striped in turquoise, black and red, endangered and found only on the San Francisco Peninsula. It hunts the California red-legged frog, itself protected, in the wet margins. Chorus frogs supply the noise. Look from the dam and stay on it.

{{PHOTO:pelican}}
Pelicans and the birds of the islandpelicans most of the year · seabirds nest in spring and summer

Brown pelicans pass the point in low lines over the swell and are the most recorded bird in the park. The island holds the largest and most varied seabird colony in the Monterey Bay sanctuary: Brandt’s and pelagic cormorants, western gulls, pigeon guillemots, black oystercatchers and two kinds of auklet. In spring and summer bank swallows, a threatened species in California, nest in the sandy cliffs of the point and feed low over the dunes.

{{PHOTO:island}}
Sea lionsall year, most from May to August · the island, from 4

The barking is California sea lions: dark brown, with small ear flaps, walking on their flippers up the rocks and through the rooms of the keeper’s house. They have bred here since about 2010, with 800 to 1,000 pups a year. The bigger, pale tawny animals on the outer rocks are Steller sea lions, which roar instead of barking. A fog signal was put on the island in 1872 and a light tower in 1890; the keepers left in 1948.

{{PHOTO:seal}}
Harbour seals, otters and whalesin the water off 4 to 6

A round, spotted, earless head watching you from the surf is a harbour seal, small beside everything else on this point; they pup on the island in April and May. Sea otters, floating on their backs in the kelp, are seen now and then: they came back to this coast in the early 1980s. In spring grey whales pass close to the point on their way north, and the sign of one is a low, bushy spout.

A point that lost its tip

{{PHOTO:coast}}
Terraces, a fault and a dune fieldthe whole route

You walk on two flat benches cut by surf when the sea stood higher, the first about 100,000 years old and the second about 80,000; the short climb after the pond crosses the fault that lifted one above the other. When Sebastián Vizcaíno named the point for the New Year in 1603, the park says, it still reached as far as the island. The sea cut the channel in the century after. Sand that used to pile up against the point now passes through the gap, so the dunes are slowly starving and the scrub is closing over them. The rock at the water’s edge is thinly layered mudstone, 12 to 13 million years old.

Getting to Año Nuevo

Highway 1 from Half Moon Bay to the park: 44 km (27 miles), about 40 minutes. Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

The entrance is on Highway 1, on the ocean side, between Half Moon Bay and Santa Cruz. The whole drive is paved.

From Santa Cruz the park is about 32 km (20 mi) north on Highway 1, on the left, past Davenport.

Practical

  • Check before you leave home. The park closes the seal area when it has to. In February 2026 bird flu in the pups ended the guided walks for the rest of that season. Park page: parks.ca.gov, Año Nuevo State Park; office +1 650 879 2025.
  • Toilets at the car park and pit toilets at the staging area 3. Carry your own water.
  • Weather: cool, windy and often foggy in summer; cold, wet and windy on a winter walk. Hooded waterproof, warm layers, shoes that can take mud and sand.
  • Rules: no dogs anywhere in the park, even left in the car. No drones, no bicycles on trails, no smoking. No umbrellas in the seal area, and no food on a guided walk. Take nothing away.
  • Poison oak (glossy leaves in threes, red in autumn) lines the New Years Creek Trail. Check for ticks after brushing through grass.
  • If you cannot manage the sand: the park runs van tours to the boardwalk at 5 for visitors who need them. Book by phone on +1 800 444 4445.

Afterwards

  • Highway 1 Brewing Company, 5720 Cabrillo Highway, a few minutes north of the park. A roadside brew pub with a food menu; children and dogs welcome. Friday to Sunday, noon to 6 pm. +1 650 879 9243.
  • Duarte’s Tavern, Stage Road, Pescadero B. In the same family since 1894: cream of artichoke soup and local fish. Lunch from 11 am, to 3 pm on Monday, Wednesday and Thursday and to 6 pm from Friday to Sunday. Closed Tuesday. +1 650 879 0464.
  • Arcangeli Grocery, along the street from Duarte’s: sandwiches, and the garlic, herb and artichoke bread the bakery is known for. 11 am to 5 pm, closed Tuesday.
  • Davenport Roadhouse, on Highway 1 in Davenport, about 17 km (11 mi) south. Café from 9 am, an all-day menu and a bar, seven days a week.
  • Swanton Berry Farm, about 14 km (9 mi) south: a farm stand with pie, cobbler, shortcake and jam. Daily, 8 am to 5 pm.
