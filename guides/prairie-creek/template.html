A full-day hike · Prairie Creek Redwoods State Park, northern California

Fern Canyon the long way

Leave the car beside a meadow of grazing elk and walk {{u:seven kilometres|four and a half miles}} through old-growth redwoods to a canyon whose walls are fern from the creek to the rim. Wade down it to a beach where elk walk the sand, then climb home over a second ridge of redwoods. It is a long day, and your feet get wet.

{{PHOTO:hero}}

Why this hike

Most people reach Fern Canyon by car: {{u:13 km|8 miles}} of single-lane gravel with two streams to drive through, a $12 fee, and from {{u:15 May to 15 September|May 15 to September 15}} a reservation that often sells out. They park, walk the canyon and drive back. Hikers who come from the visitor centre need no reservation and pay nothing, and the walk in is the park's classic redwood trail.

  • The redwoods. The park holds over {{u:4,000 hectares|10,000 acres}} of old growth, the second-largest unbroken stand left anywhere. The James Irvine Trail runs through the middle of it for two hours.
  • Fern Canyon. A slot {{u:15 m|50 feet}} deep, its walls hung with seven kinds of fern and running with water. You walk in the creek.
  • Elk. Roosevelt elk graze the meadow at the trailhead and the dunes at Gold Bluffs Beach. Bulls weigh up to {{u:540 kg|1,200 lb}} (page 4).
  • Dinner in Trinidad, a fishing village on a headland, forty minutes down the coast (page 5).

Is this for me?

Yes, if you can walk all day. Nothing here is steep for long or hard to follow; it is the distance that tires people.

EffortLike 25 km on the flat. The trail rolls in and out of side valleys all day, and the one long climb, {{u:190 m|620 ft}} up Miners Ridge, comes after the beach, when you are tired.
UnderfootSoft forest path with roots and steps. In the canyon: cobbles, logs to climb over, and water ankle to knee deep. Footbridges go in from June to September and you still wade: carry sandals or spare socks.
WhenMay to October. Winter rain can flood the canyon, and storms close roads and trails.
ShorterDrive to the Fern Canyon car park and walk the {{u:1.6 km|one-mile}} canyon loop (page 3).

Keep {{u:25 metres|25 yards}} from elk, and more in May, June and from late August to October, when cows with calves and bulls in the rut charge people (page 4).

Why this way round

Out by the James Irvine Trail puts the biggest trees in the morning, when you are fresh enough to look at them. It also brings you to Fern Canyon from the top, so you walk down the creek with the walls growing taller, and step out of the canyon mouth onto the beach.

When to start

The park is open from sunrise to sunset. In summer, start by 9 am: that puts you in the canyon around noon and back at the car by 5, with time to drive to Trinidad for dinner. From October to March the days are short; start by 8 and carry a headtorch.

The trails start beside the building. Follow the signs for the James Irvine Trail over the footbridge across Prairie Creek and through several junctions in the first few hundred metres; every one is signed.

For the next {{u:4 km|2.5 miles}} it contours along the side of a creek valley through the heart of the old growth: big redwoods standing well apart over a floor of fern and sorrel, with fallen trunks beside the path that you cannot see over. This is the stretch to walk slowly.
35.2 km92 m Clintonia Trail, on the left: the link to Miners Ridge, {{u:2.4 km|1.5 miles}} away. Keep straight on. The trail now works down towards the coast, and the forest changes as you near the sea: the redwoods thin out and Sitka spruce takes over. At {{u:7.3 km|4.6 miles}} the Friendship Ridge Trail comes in on the right; keep left.
47.7 km36 m The head of Fern Canyon. Stairs on the left drop into the creek bed. Turn downstream and walk in the water. For {{u:600 m|a third of a mile}} the walls stand straight up on both sides, covered from top to bottom in five-finger fern, with water dripping through it; the only sound is the creek. Expect to climb over log jams left by the winter floods. If the stairs are closed, stay on the trail along the rim to 5 and walk up the canyon from its mouth instead.
5{{u:8.4 km|5.2 mi}}9 m The canyon mouth. A path leads {{u:400 m|a quarter of a mile}} to the Fern Canyon car park (vault toilets, picnic tables). The beach is straight ahead through the dunes: Gold Bluffs Beach, wide and grey, under ochre cliffs. In May 1850 prospectors saw gold in this sand; washing it out proved too much work and they moved on. Turn south and walk {{u:2.2 km|1.4 miles}} along the beach or on the gravel road behind it. Elk feed in the dune grass and stand in the road here; give them room.
610.5 km10 m Gold Bluffs Beach campground. The Miners Ridge Trail is signed on the inland side of the road. It starts on an old forest road through trees that have grown back since logging, then climbs steeply up a side ravine where the old redwoods return, bigger and closer together than anywhere else on the loop, and the light drops.
713.9 km146 m Clintonia Trail joins from the left. Keep right. The trail runs level along the ridge top for {{u:1.5 km|a mile}}, through drier, airier forest, to the high point of the day at {{u:197 m|650 ft}}, then drops.
216.6 km108 m Back at the fork. Turn right and retrace the first {{u:1.4 km|0.9 miles}}.
117.9 km45 m The visitor centre. Look over the meadow again before you drive off.

Home by the Clintonia Trail. While Miners Ridge is blocked between 6 and 7, see the canyon and the beach from 5, then climb the steps beside the canyon mouth and walk back up the James Irvine Trail to 3. Turn right onto the Clintonia Trail, which climbs {{u:2.4 km|1.5 miles}} through old growth to Miners Ridge at 7; turn left there for the visitor centre. It comes to 17.7 km and the same climb, plus whatever you walk in the canyon and on the sand.

The short way: drive to the canyon. Davison Road leaves Highway 101 {{u:5 km|3 miles}} north of Orick: {{u:13 km|8 miles}} of single-lane gravel with two streams to drive through. Low cars get stuck in them, and nothing over {{u:7.3 m|24 feet}} long or towing a trailer is allowed. It costs $12 a car, cash or cheque. From {{u:15 May to 15 September|May 15 to September 15}} you also need a free reservation, made at least a day ahead through the Redwood Parks Conservancy; they go quickly. From the car park the canyon loop is {{u:1.6 km|one mile}}: up the creek, out by the stairs, and back along the rim.

Field notes

Who lives here

{{PHOTO:elk_beach}}
Roosevelt elkall year · Elk Prairie 1, Gold Bluffs Beach 5 to 6

The largest elk in North America, dark-necked and pale-rumped, and the animal you are most likely to meet: visitors have logged them here more than a thousand times. The park's herds run from ten to fifty animals. One uses the meadow by the visitor centre; another lives along Gold Bluffs Beach and is often around the canyon mouth. They also walk the forest trails, so you may come round a bend onto one.

{{PHOTO:elk_bull}}
The rut and the calveslate August to October · late May and June

From late August to October the bulls gather cows into harems and bugle, a rising squeal that carries across the meadow at dusk. They stamp and charge at people and at cars. In late May and June cows hide newborn calves in the grass, sometimes beside a trail, and will rear and strike with their front legs. The park asks for {{u:25 metres|25 yards}} at all times and more in those months. Meet a bull with his cows on the trail: back away slowly and find another way. Approaching elk is against state law.

{{PHOTO:slug}}
On the forest floorall day, most after rain

The banana slug, yellow and as long as your hand, has more visitors' records here than the elk. Look on the path and on wet logs. The rough-skinned newt, brown above and orange below, walks about in daylight because its skin is poisonous. Under bark and fallen wood lives the ensatina, a small brown salamander with orange at the base of each leg. Look, and leave them where they are.

{{PHOTO:frog}}
In the creekFern Canyon 4 to 5, and every stream you cross

The northern red-legged frog sits at the water's edge in the canyon, brown or brick-red with red under its legs. The coastal giant salamander, mottled like marble and up to {{u:30 cm|a foot}} long, lives in the streams; its young stay in the pools. On the rocks, watch for the American dipper, a plump grey bird that bobs up and down and then walks under the water to feed.

If an elk is close. Stay together. Do not stare at it. Walk widely round, keeping it in view, and make some noise so it knows where you are. Watch for a lowered head, a flicking tongue or pawing at the ground. If it charges, get behind a tree or up one, and drop your pack or jacket in front of you as a decoy. Tell a ranger afterwards. (The park's advice.)

Birds, heard more than seen. The loud blue-black bird with a crest is Steller's jay. The single long whistle from the treetops is the varied thrush; the tiny brown bird pouring out song from a root tangle is the Pacific wren. Brown pelicans fly the surf line. Marbled murrelets, seabirds that nest high in old redwoods, are here and seldom seen; jays and ravens rob their nests, which is why the park asks you to leave no crumbs.

Also here: black-tailed deer, Douglas squirrels and chipmunks along the trail; grey foxes and black bears, each recorded a few dozen times; harbour seals in the surf off the beach.

A canyon of ferns, a forest that was bought

{{PHOTO:canyon2}}
The canyon4 to 5

The bluffs behind the beach are sand and gravel laid down by rivers and the sea, soft enough for Home Creek to cut a slot straight down through them. Water seeps out of the walls all year, and the ferns root in the wet face: five-finger fern above all, with deer, lady, sword and chain ferns among it. The film-makers of The Lost World: Jurassic Park used it for a dinosaur scene.

{{PHOTO:ridge}}
The forest2 to 3, 6 to 7

Coast redwoods are the tallest trees on Earth. Between 1880 and the early 1900s thousands of acres of them were cut to the bare ground. The Save the Redwoods League, founded in 1918, and the State of California bought the stands along Prairie Creek, and by 1923 they were a park. The men of the Civilian Conservation Corps camped on Elk Prairie in the 1930s and built the visitor centre and the trails you are walking. The park lies on the land of the Yurok people, who live here still.

Getting to the visitor centre

{{ACCESS}}

Highway 101 from Orick to the visitor centre: 10 km (6 miles). Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

The park is on Highway 101, {{u:80 km|50 miles}} north of Eureka and {{u:40 km|25 miles}} south of Crescent City. The road is paved to the door; any car is fine.

AOrick, the last village, 33 km (20 miles) north of Trinidad on Highway 101. Keep north.
BDavison Road turns off on the left for Gold Bluffs Beach. Keep on the highway.
CLeave the highway at Exit 753, signed Newton B. Drury Scenic Parkway, and turn left onto the Parkway.
PAfter {{u:1.9 km|1.2 miles}}, turn left to the visitor centre, beside the Elk Prairie campground.

From the north, leave Highway 101 at the top of the Parkway and drive almost all of its {{u:16 km|10 miles}} through the redwoods; the visitor centre is at the south end.

Parking and the Parkway

Parking is free by the visitor centre and along the Parkway. The spaces by the building are few; if they are full, park on the Parkway shoulder.

The Parkway shuts to cars in storms and high winds, and on the first Saturday of each month from October to May. Exit 753 and the visitor centre stay reachable.

Practical

  • Water: start with 2 litres each. Creek water needs treating.
  • Toilets at the visitor centre and at the Fern Canyon car park 5.
  • Weather: cool and damp all year. Summer mornings are foggy and the fog often clears by midday; bring a waterproof in any month.
  • Wind: big trees drop branches. The park closes trails in storms; stay out of the forest in a gale.
  • Rules: no dogs on any trail. Stay on the path. Take nothing and leave no food scraps.
  • Park office: +1 707 464 6101. Emergency: 911.

Afterwards: Trinidad

{{PHOTO:trinidad}}

Forty minutes south, a village of a few streets on a headland above a harbour of fishing boats. Walk down to the pier while the light lasts, then eat.

  • The Larrupin' Cafe, in the woods on Patrick's Point Drive north of the village: fish, ribs and brisket grilled over mesquite, with the bread and sauces made in the house. The reward for an 18 km day; change your shoes. Daily from 4:30 pm, 4 pm Friday to Sunday; last seating 8 pm. Book: +1 707 677 0230.
  • Trinidad Bay Eatery & Gallery, 607 Parker Street, in the village: burgers, fish and a patio, boots welcome. Open until 8 pm daily; no reservations. +1 707 677 3777.
  • Seascape, on the pier, is for breakfast on the way up: open 8 am to 4 pm.
