Why not just drive to Muir Woods?

You can, and nearly a million people a year do. But the famous grove is the bottom of a much bigger, much emptier forest, and the best way to see it is to arrive on foot from above. Same redwoods, a completely different day.

The front door
  • A parking or shuttle reservation is always required, for a timed slot, booked ahead on gomuirwoods.com. Popular days sell out.
  • A winding drive to the valley floor, then a paved path and boardwalk shared with tour-bus groups.
  • Most visitors walk the 1.5 km loop, take the photo and leave within the hour.
  • You see one thing: big trees, from a boardwalk.
The back door — this hike
  • No reservation, free parking at the trailhead opposite the Inn, or take the bus that stops at its door.
  • Forty minutes down through silent Douglas-fir forest, and you step out of the trees straight into the old growth.
  • Walk upstream through Cathedral Grove. The crowd thins with every bridge, and where the boardwalk visitors turn round, you keep going.
  • Climb out through a fern canyon beside a tumbling creek, cross a sunny meadow, and finish on a deck above the fog with a drink in your hand.

The honest trade-off: it costs a morning, not an hour, and every metre you walk down you must walk back up.

Is this for me? An honest fitness check

If you can jog 5 km without stopping, or cycle 40–50 km at touring pace, your heart and lungs will be fine. Your legs are another matter: flat-country running and cycling do not train them for this much down and up. If you walk in the Sierra de Guadarrama from Madrid, relax — this is the easier day.

Overall effortAbout the same as a brisk 13 km walk on the flat (every 100 m of climbing costs about an extra kilometre): 2½ hours of walking plus stops. The climb totals roughly 350 m — more than sea level to the top of the Vaalserberg (322 m), a little over half of Puerto de Cotos to Peñalara — and most of it comes in one piece, in the middle.
The descent (km 0.7–3.0)240 m down in 2.4 km on the Canopy View Trail: an even 10% on switchbacks, the gentlest way down there is. Still 40 minutes of braking work for thighs and knees; you may feel it on the stairs next morning.
The climb out (km 4.2–6.3)250 m up in 2.1 km on the Bootjack Trail, an average 12%, much of it on log and stone steps: 45–55 minutes of continuous uphill, similar to climbing the stairs of the Dom Tower two and a half times, slowly. A steady pace and a few short pauses do it. It is shaded and beside the creek, and afterwards the last 3 km are easy: level or gently downhill, with a final 50 m rise on a paved lane.
No easy way outOnce at the bottom the only way back is up, and with no mobile signal you cannot call a taxi. If in doubt take the short version (page 3), or turn round on the way down. Paths are narrow, rooty dirt, sometimes muddy; no scrambling, nothing that needs hands. Wear shoes with grip.

Why this direction? The steep, stepped trail is climbed and the smooth one descended; steps are far kinder going up than coming down. Who should think twice: anyone with knee trouble, or who rarely walks more than an hour. Who will find it easy: anyone who hikes in hills a few times a year.

Step by step

Junctions are well signed with wooden posts giving trail names and distances in miles. Signs are the reason the trail names below matter: follow the names, not the other hikers.

The short version — 6.4 km (4.0 mi), about 260 m of climbing, 2–2½ hours

Same descent, same redwoods, but a shorter way out that skips the Bootjack canyon, the meadow and Troop 80. Follow the main route to point 5. There turn right onto the Fern Creek Trail and follow the creek for 600 m, then turn right onto the Lost Trail, which climbs 150 m in 800 m on a long series of steps — short, but the steepest thing in this guide. At the top you rejoin the Canopy View Trail at point 3: turn left, uphill, and retrace your steps past point 2 along the Panoramic Trail to the start.

Choose this if time or daylight is short. Choose the main loop if you can: the Bootjack canyon is the most beautiful part of the day, and the walk back along Troop 80 is the easiest.

Getting to the Mountain Home Inn

Driving route from the Golden Gate Bridge: 19 km (12 miles). Basemap: USGS The National Map. Route: OpenStreetMap via OSRM.

Parking

The trailhead car park is free and needs no reservation, but it is small and fills early on fine weekends. If it is full, there is free roadside parking on the shoulder of Panoramic Highway on either side of the Inn. Park with all four wheels outside the white line, obey any no-parking signs, and take care crossing the road. Arrive before 9:00 at weekends, or come on a weekday.

Without a car

Marin Transit route 61 stops at the Inn’s door, a few times a day, from Sausalito, Marin City and Mill Valley. Check the timetable for the last bus back.

Driving notes

Highway 1 and Panoramic Highway are narrow and twisting, with cyclists on the climbs; 22 minutes on paper often takes longer. Returning to San Francisco, the bridge toll is collected southbound by number-plate camera; a rental company will bill it to you.

To Mill Valley for dinner: go back down to the four-way junction (E) and turn left on Sequoia Valley Road, which descends into the town centre in about 10 minutes.

Practical

When to go

Any time of year. Muir Woods is open from 8:00 until about sunset (17:00 in winter, as late as 20:00 in summer), and Mount Tamalpais State Park closes at sunset too. In winter start by 11:00 at the latest; 9:00 is ideal, and the grove is at its quietest before 10:00. Fog is common and suits the redwoods. November to March is the rainy season: expect mud, a louder creek, and from December perhaps spawning salmon in it.

Fees

Walking in is allowed, and no parking reservation is needed if you do not park at Muir Woods. The entrance fee still applies: US$15 per person aged 16 and over, payable at the visitor centre (the detour at point 4). A US national-parks pass covers it.

Bring

  • 1 litre of water each. Taps at the trailhead and the Muir Woods visitor centre; nothing in between.
  • Shoes with grip, and a warm layer: the canyon floor can be 8–10 °C cooler than the ridge.
  • The route downloaded for offline use, and a payment card.
  • A snack for Van Wyck Meadow. Picnicking is not allowed inside Muir Woods itself.

Good to know

  • No mobile signal in Muir Woods or most of the canyon.
  • Toilets: at the trailhead and at the visitor centre.
  • No dogs, bikes or drones on these trails. Stay on the path — the redwood roots are shallow.
  • Poison oak grows beside the trails: a shrub with glossy leaves in threes, red in autumn. Touching it gives an itchy rash a day later. Just do not brush against the greenery.
  • Check for ticks afterwards. Rattlesnakes and mountain lions live here but are very rarely seen.
  • Car break-ins happen at trailheads. Leave nothing visible in the car — not even a jacket.
  • Emergency: 911. Park rangers: +1 415 388 2070.

Afterwards: where to eat

At the finish — Mountain Home Inn. The whole point of ending here: a wooden deck on the ridge looking over Mill Valley, the bay and, on a clear day, the East Bay hills. Hikers have been stopping here for over a century. Food is casual (one recent report says counter service rather than waiters) and opening hours vary by season, so check before you count on lunch: +1 415 381 9000. Thursday is “locals night”, 17:00–20:00, with live music.

Down in Mill Valley, 10–15 minutes’ drive, a small town in a redwood canyon with a walkable centre around Throckmorton Avenue and Miller Avenue. Names are links to each restaurant’s website. Coming from Madrid, note that Americans eat early: most kitchens close around 21:00, so think of dinner at 18:30–19:30.
